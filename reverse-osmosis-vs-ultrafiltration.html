Reverse Osmosis vs Ultrafiltration for Microplastics — A Plain-English Comparison

Both block microplastics easily. The real differences are cost, water waste, and what each system does to the rest of your water.

Published: February 15, 2025 8 min read
Sources U.S. EPA NSF / ANSI Env. Sci. & Tech. WHO

TL;DR: Both reverse osmosis (RO) and ultrafiltration (UF) block microplastics easily — the pore sizes are far smaller than any microplastic particle. The real difference is what else they do: RO strips almost everything, including minerals, and wastes water; UF keeps minerals and runs at full pressure.

Short version: Both reverse osmosis (RO) and ultrafiltration (UF) remove microplastics — the question isn't whether they work, it's what else they change about your water. RO strips almost everything (minerals, fluoride, dissolved solids) and wastes 2–4 gallons per gallon produced. UF keeps minerals, runs at city water pressure, and costs less to own. Pick RO if your tap water has heavy metals or high TDS; pick UF if microplastics, bacteria, and sediment are the main concern.

The 30-Second Answer

For microplastics alone, both technologies are overkill — in a good way. Microplastics are defined as plastic fragments between 1 micron and 5 millimeters. RO membranes filter down to roughly 0.0001 micron. UF membranes filter down to about 0.01 micron. Even the smallest microplastic particle is 100 times bigger than what a UF membrane lets through, and 10,000 times bigger than what an RO membrane lets through.

So the comparison isn't really about microplastics. It's about cost, water waste, mineral content, and what other contaminants live in your specific tap water. How Reverse Osmosis Works

RO pushes water under pressure through a semi-permeable membrane with pores so tight that only water molecules and a handful of similarly-sized molecules pass through. Everything else — dissolved salts, heavy metals, pharmaceuticals, microplastics, nanoplastics — gets pushed to a reject stream and flushed down the drain.

A typical home RO unit has four to five stages: a sediment pre-filter, one or two carbon blocks, the RO membrane itself, and a post-filter polishing carbon. Some units add a remineralization stage to put calcium and magnesium back, because RO water is essentially demineralized and tastes flat to many people. The U.S. EPA lists RO as one of the approved methods for removing arsenic, lead, and other inorganic contaminants from drinking water, which is exactly the use case where it shines — when your tap water has problems beyond microplastics.

How Ultrafiltration Works

UF also uses a membrane, but the pores are about 100 times larger than RO. That sounds like a downside until you look at what those pores actually block: particles, sediment, microplastics, bacteria, protozoan cysts like Giardia and Cryptosporidium, and most colloidal matter. What UF lets through are dissolved minerals and most dissolved chemicals — including chlorine, fluoride, and heavy metals.

UF runs on city water pressure. There's no booster pump, no electricity, and no reject stream. Whatever water goes in comes out clean — minus what the membrane caught. For microplastic removal specifically, UF is the more elegant answer because it solves the problem without solving twenty other problems you don't have. Microplastic Removal Head-to-Head

Independent testing tells the same story. Studies summarized in our Filter Pore Size Chart: Microns, Nanometers & What They Block show that any filter at or below 1 micron removes essentially all detectable microplastic particles. A 0.01-micron UF membrane and a 0.0001-micron RO membrane are both far below that threshold.

The one edge RO has is on the nanoplastic end. Nanoplastics — particles smaller than 1 micron — can be a few hundred nanometers wide. That's still bigger than a UF pore, so UF catches most of them, but not all. RO captures essentially 100%. If you've read the Microplastics in Human Blood: What the 2024-2025 Studies Found studies and want maximum certainty on the smallest particles, RO has a measurable advantage.

What Else Each System Removes

This is where the two technologies stop being interchangeable.

RO removes about 95–99% of total dissolved solids (TDS): lead, arsenic, fluoride, nitrates, chromium-6, PFAS, sodium, calcium, magnesium. If you're on well water or in a city with documented contamination problems, RO is genuinely useful. The downside is that it removes the good minerals along with the bad, which is why many users add a remineralization cartridge or drink mineral-rich foods to compensate.

UF removes particles and microbes but leaves dissolved minerals and chemicals intact. To remove chlorine, taste, or organic compounds, UF systems pair with an activated carbon stage — which is why our Activated Carbon vs. Ultrafiltration: Which Removes More? comparison matters. Most real-world UF home systems are actually UF + carbon, giving you sediment, microplastic, microbial, and taste protection without touching minerals.

Water Waste and Running Cost

RO's reputation for wasting water is earned. Older units flushed 4 gallons of brine for every gallon of drinkable water. Modern permeate-pump and tankless RO systems have improved that to roughly 1:1, but it's still water down the drain. Over a year, a family of four on RO can send 5,000–10,000 gallons to waste depending on the unit.

UF wastes zero water during normal operation. You only lose a small amount during periodic backflushing, which most systems do automatically. On a 10-year ownership horizon, the water bill difference between RO and UF in a high-tier-pricing city like San Diego or Atlanta can run several hundred dollars.

Filter replacement is closer. RO membranes last 2–3 years and cost $50–$100. Pre- and post-filters need swapping every 6–12 months. UF membranes typically last 1–2 years and cost $30–$60. Carbon stages on either system need yearly replacement.

Installation, Space and Practicality

Both systems usually live under the kitchen sink with a dedicated faucet drilled through the countertop. RO needs a storage tank because the membrane produces water slowly — a tankless RO uses a higher-capacity membrane plus a small pump instead. UF runs on demand at full flow and doesn't need a tank.

For renters, neither is ideal. Drilling a faucet hole isn't a simple reversible upgrade. Countertop versions exist for both technologies but require some plumbing work or hose adapters. This is where on-the-go options come in: if your goal is microplastic-free water at work, the gym, on a flight, or at a hotel, a cap-style filter that screws onto your existing bottle solves the problem without installation. Which Should You Buy?

Pick RO if at least one of these is true: you have well water, your municipal report shows elevated lead or arsenic, you live in an area with PFAS contamination, your TDS reading is over 500 ppm, or you simply want the most thorough filtration money can buy and don't mind the water waste.

Pick UF if: your tap water tests safe for heavy metals, you want to keep minerals in your drinking water, you hate the idea of water waste, you have low water pressure that an RO booster would struggle with, or microplastics and microbes are your main concerns.

Pick a Bottle Cap Water Filters: How They Work or another portable solution if: your microplastic exposure mostly comes from bottled water rather than tap, you travel often, you don't own the place you live in, or you simply don't want to commit to under-sink plumbing. A 0.1-micron cap filter blocks microplastics at the same threshold UF does — just at the bottle instead of at the faucet. Where Cap Filters Fit

RO and UF solve a tap-water problem. They don't solve the bottled-water problem documented in the 2024 Columbia/Rutgers nanoplastic study, where a liter of bottled water averaged 240,000 plastic fragments. If you drink bottled water at work, on flights, or at the gym, an under-sink system at home doesn't reach that water. Cap filters do.

This isn't an either/or. Many households run UF at home for cooking and cooking water, and use a cap filter for bottled water on the move. That's the cleanest combination for total microplastic exposure without overpaying.

Bottom Line

Reverse osmosis is the broader tool — it removes everything, including minerals and a lot of your water. Ultrafiltration is the surgical tool — it removes microplastics, sediment, and microbes while leaving the chemistry of your water alone. For microplastic removal specifically, UF is the more efficient choice. For comprehensive contamination control, RO wins. For microplastics in the bottles you actually drink from outside the house, neither system reaches the water — that's where cap filters come in. Match the tool to the problem, not to the marketing.

Per the EPA Drinking Water Treatability Database, both technologies are well-characterized and reliable for the contaminants they target. The decision is about your water, your budget, and where you actually drink.

FAQ

Does reverse osmosis remove microplastics?

Yes. RO membranes filter to about 0.0001 micron, which is roughly 10,000 times smaller than the smallest microplastic particle. Independent tests show essentially complete removal of microplastics and most nanoplastics.

Is ultrafiltration enough for microplastics?

Yes. UF membranes filter to about 0.01 micron, well below the 1-micron microplastic threshold. UF removes 100% of detectable microplastics while keeping minerals in the water, which is why many homes prefer it over RO.

How much water does RO waste compared to UF?

Older RO systems waste 2 to 4 gallons per gallon of filtered water. Modern tankless RO has improved that to roughly 1:1. Ultrafiltration wastes essentially no water during normal operation, only a small amount during periodic backflushing.

Which is cheaper to run long-term, RO or UF?

UF is cheaper to own. Lower upfront cost ($80 to $250 vs $200 to $600), no booster pump, no wasted water, and cheaper replacement membranes. RO is only worth the extra cost if you actually need heavy-metal or TDS removal.

Do I need RO or UF if I mostly drink bottled water?

Neither. RO and UF are under-sink systems that filter tap water. They don't touch the bottled water you buy at the store or drink at the gym. A cap-style bottle filter is the right tool for that exposure path.

Can RO and UF be used together?

Yes. Some premium systems chain a UF stage as a pre-filter before the RO membrane to extend membrane life. For most households this is overkill — pick one technology based on your tap water profile.
