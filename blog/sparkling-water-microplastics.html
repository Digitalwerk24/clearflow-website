Sparkling Water and Microplastics: Are Carbonated Brands Different?

Carbonation, pressure and acidity together change how plastic bottles behave — here's what testing shows about fizzy water

February 15, 2025 8 min read Sources: Stiftung Warentest, Uni Mainz

TL;DR: Sparkling water sold in plastic bottles tends to contain microplastics at levels similar to — and sometimes higher than — still bottled water. Carbonation, pressure, and acidity all play a role. Glass-bottled fizzy brands generally test better.

Kurz: If you drink fizzy water from a plastic bottle, you are almost certainly drinking microplastics too. Several independent studies have found that carbonated bottled water often contains as many — or more — plastic particles per liter than still water. The good news: switching containers and adding a simple cap-style filter can cut exposure significantly without giving up the bubbles.

Carbonated water has a loyal following in the United States. LaCroix, Topo Chico, Perrier, San Pellegrino, Bubly and dozens of store brands fill grocery aisles, and many drinkers reach for them as a "cleaner" alternative to soda. That assumption is mostly fair on sugar and calories. On microplastics, it falls apart. This guide reviews the available data on plastic particles in sparkling water, explains why bubbles and plastic interact in unhelpful ways, and shows what you can do without giving up the fizz. For broader context on bottle types and brand testing, see our pillar on Plastic Bottles, Brands & Smarter Drinking Choices.

Why sparkling water gets its own conversation

Most microplastic studies group all bottled water together. That hides an important detail. Sparkling water sits inside its container under pressure, often for months. The liquid is mildly acidic — carbon dioxide dissolved in water forms carbonic acid, which pulls the pH down to roughly 3.5 to 4.5 depending on the brand. Acidic, pressurized liquid is a more aggressive environment for plastic packaging than flat, neutral spring water. That is the core reason researchers and consumers have started asking whether sparkling water microplastics deserve a separate look.

It is also a category dominated by plastic. While Perrier and San Pellegrino are best known in glass, the volume sellers in American grocery stores — LaCroix, Bubly, Polar, Spindrift, store-brand seltzers — almost all ship in PET plastic cans or bottles, or in aluminum cans with a plastic liner. That is the format most shoppers buy by the case.

What the studies actually found

The most widely cited research on plastic particles in bottled water is the 2018 State University of New York at Fredonia study led by Sherri Mason, and the 2024 Columbia University study using hyperspectral imaging, both covered in detail on our Microplastics in Bottled Water: What the Studies Actually Show page. Neither study singled out sparkling brands, but several follow-up tests by European consumer groups and academic labs did.

A 2018 investigation by the German consumer magazine Stiftung Warentest analyzed 32 mineral waters, including carbonated brands sold in both PET and glass. Plastic particles were found in every single sample. Carbonated water in single-use PET bottles showed the highest median particle counts in that test set. Glass-bottled sparkling water still contained particles, but at much lower levels. A 2020 study by the University of Mainz reached similar conclusions: reusable PET sparkling bottles released more particles than single-use ones, and glass returnables were lowest.

The takeaway is consistent: carbonation plus plastic packaging tends to produce higher counts than still water in the same material. Sources for the underlying research are summarized by the Wikipedia overview of microplastics, which links to the original journal articles.

How carbonation moves plastic into your drink

Three mechanisms are at work, and they stack.

  1. Pressure. A sealed bottle of sparkling water holds roughly 2 to 4 atmospheres of pressure. That constant force presses liquid against the bottle wall and cap liner. Over weeks of storage, tiny amounts of plastic abrade or migrate.
  2. Acidity. Carbonic acid is mild but persistent. It accelerates the breakdown of plasticizers and surface polymers, especially when the bottle is warm.
  3. Bubble mechanics. Gas bubbles nucleate on imperfections in the inner bottle wall. As they form and rise, they can carry loose surface fragments into the water column — effectively scrubbing micro-fragments off the plastic.

None of these effects are dramatic in isolation. Combined, and held at room temperature for months on a warehouse shelf, they explain why carbonated PET bottles tend to test higher than still PET bottles of the same brand.

Aluminum cans with plastic liners: a quiet middle ground

LaCroix, Bubly and most American seltzers ship in aluminum cans. The aluminum itself does not shed microplastic. The thin epoxy or polymer liner that coats the inside of the can does — but the contact time is short, since cans are usually consumed within weeks of filling rather than sitting for a year. Liner technology has also shifted away from BPA, though as we cover in BPA-Free Bottles and Microplastics: Is It Actually Safer?, BPA-free is not the same as plastic-free.

Expect canned sparkling water to fall between glass and PET on particle count. That matches the limited testing available.

Does the fizz disappear if you filter it?

A common question: if you pour sparkling water through a filter, will it go flat? The honest answer is mostly yes. Pouring fizzy water across a porous medium accelerates degassing — bubbles form on the filter surface and escape. A hollow-fiber cap-style filter, like the kind we cover in Filter Bottles vs Screw-On Cap Filters, will lose some carbonation if you squeeze a full bottle through it, especially after the seal is opened.

The most practical move for fizzy-water drinkers is not "filter it after," but choose the format that already starts lower: glass when possible, cans when not, PET as a last resort.

Heat, shelf life, and storage

Two factors make any sparkling water — plastic or otherwise — worse over time. Heat accelerates migration of particles and chemicals from packaging. Long storage gives those processes time to add up. Leaving a case of fizzy PET bottles in a hot garage is the single worst thing you can do; we cover this in Hot Car Water Bottles: Does Heat Release More Plastic?. Storage length matters too, as detailed in How Long Can You Keep Bottled Water? Shelf Life Explained.

Practical choices for sparkling water drinkers

You do not have to give up bubbles. A few habits make a real difference:

  • Pick glass-bottled sparkling water when you can — Perrier, San Pellegrino, Topo Chico glass.
  • For everyday cases, cans beat PET on microplastic load.
  • Buy what you'll drink in a few weeks. Avoid stockpiling.
  • Keep cases out of cars, garages, and warm pantries.
  • Make your own with a countertop carbonator using filtered tap water — the cheapest long-term path to low-microplastic fizz.

Bottom line

Sparkling water is not a microplastic-free alternative to still water — it is often the opposite when it comes in PET. The combination of pressure, carbonic acid and bubble action makes plastic packaging shed more, not less. Choose glass where you can, cans where you can't, and reserve PET sparkling for occasional use. For your regular hydration, a cap-style filter on the still bottled water you already buy is the simplest way to drink less plastic without changing your routine.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does sparkling water have more microplastics than still water?

In most published tests, carbonated water in PET plastic bottles contained as many or more microplastic particles per liter than still water in the same material. Pressure, acidity, and bubble action together pull more particles from the plastic into the liquid over time.

Is LaCroix safe from microplastics?

LaCroix is sold in aluminum cans with a thin polymer liner, so it sits between glass and PET on expected microplastic load. There is no public peer-reviewed brand ranking for LaCroix specifically, but cans generally test lower than plastic bottles because contact time is shorter.

Is glass-bottled sparkling water better?

Yes. European consumer-group testing has consistently shown glass-bottled sparkling water, including Perrier and San Pellegrino in glass, at significantly lower particle counts than the same or similar brands in PET. Glass does not shed plastic into the water.

Can I filter sparkling water through a cap filter?

You can, but the carbonation will largely escape. Filter media accelerate bubble formation, so the water goes flat as it passes through. A cap filter is best used on still bottled water; for fizzy drinks, choosing glass or cans up front is the more practical fix.

Why does carbonation increase microplastic release?

Carbonated water is under pressure and slightly acidic because dissolved carbon dioxide forms carbonic acid. Pressure presses water against the bottle wall, acidity slowly degrades the polymer surface, and rising bubbles can dislodge loose fragments. All three effects together raise particle counts compared to still water in the same bottle.

Does making sparkling water at home avoid microplastics?

Largely yes, if you start with filtered tap water and use a glass or stainless carbonator bottle. The CO2 itself is plastic-free, and you skip the months of warehouse storage that drive most plastic migration in commercial bottles.
