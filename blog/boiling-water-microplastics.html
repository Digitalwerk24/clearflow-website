Boiling Water and Microplastics: What the 2024 Study Really Showed

Boiling tap water can drop nanoplastics by up to 90% — but only in hard water, and only if you filter out the limescale afterward

February 12, 2025 8 min read Sources: Environmental Science & Technology Letters, EPA, USGS

TL;DR: Boiling tap water can drop nanoplastics by up to 90% — but only in hard water, and only if you filter out the limescale afterward. It does nothing for the bottled water you actually carry around.

Kurz: A 2024 study from Guangzhou Medical University made headlines by showing that boiling tap water can trap up to 90% of nano- and microplastic particles in limescale. The trick works — but only in hard water, only with a coffee-filter step afterward, and not at all for the sealed plastic bottle in your bag. Here is what the research actually says, where the limits sit, and what to do when you cannot boil your water.

The headline study, in plain English

In February 2024, researchers Zhanjun Li and Eddy Zeng published a short paper in Environmental Science & Technology Letters titled "Drinking Boiled Tap Water Reduces Human Intake of Nanoplastics and Microplastics." The team spiked tap water samples with known concentrations of nano- and microplastic particles (NMP), boiled them for five minutes, let them cool, and then passed the water through a basic stainless-steel mesh — the kind sitting inside most coffee makers.

The result was striking. In hard tap water, the combined boil-and-strain step removed up to 90% of the plastic particles. The mechanism is not magic: calcium and magnesium bicarbonates in hard water turn into calcium carbonate (limescale) as the water heats. The forming crystals encapsulate the plastic particles, and the resulting flakes are large enough to be caught by a coarse mesh. For more on why particle size dictates filterability, see our breakdown in Filter Pore Size Chart: Microns, Nanometers & What They Block.

The catch is buried in the same paper. In soft water — under roughly 60 mg/L of dissolved minerals — the removal rate dropped to around 25%. No scale, no co-precipitation, no trap.

Hard water vs soft water: the deciding factor

Whether boiling does anything useful depends almost entirely on what comes out of your tap. The U.S. Geological Survey classifies water hardness in milligrams of calcium carbonate per liter, and the spread across American cities is enormous.

Water type Hardness (mg/L CaCO₃) Typical US examples Boiling removal (study)
Soft 0–60 Seattle, Boston, Portland ~25%
Moderately hard 61–120 Atlanta, Houston ~50–70%
Hard 121–180 Denver, San Antonio ~80%
Very hard 180+ Phoenix, Las Vegas, Indianapolis up to 90%

If you live in the Pacific Northwest or New England, boiling will likely shave only a quarter of the plastic load off your water. If you live in the Southwest, it can be genuinely effective — provided you do the second step everyone forgets.

The step most people skip

Boiling alone does not remove anything. It only changes the form. The particles stay in the kettle, now wrapped in limescale flakes that sink, float, or stick to the kettle walls. If you pour straight from the spout into a glass, you drink them right back down.

The study used a stainless steel sieve. In a kitchen, a paper coffee filter or a fine cloth works the same way. The full sequence looks like this:

  1. Boil cold tap water at a rolling boil for at least 5 minutes.
  2. Let it cool and settle for at least 10 minutes so the scale flakes drop.
  3. Pour the water through a fine mesh, a paper coffee filter, or a cloth into a clean container — leave the last 10–15% in the kettle.
  4. Scrub the kettle. The trapped plastic is in the residue you just rinsed off.

Skip step 3 and you have done nothing except heat the same particles. Skip step 4 over time and the buildup recirculates.

Why this doesn't help the bottled water problem

Here is where the practical limit hits hard. Most people Googling "does boiling remove microplastics" are worried about the single-use bottle they bought at a gas station — the one a 2024 Columbia study found contains roughly 240,000 plastic particles per liter, mostly nanoplastics.

You cannot boil bottled water in any meaningful way:

  • The bottle itself sheds plastic when heated. PET softens around 70°C and releases additional particles and antimony. Pouring a sealed bottle into a kettle defeats the point — you'd be adding plastic from your bottled brand into a process designed to remove it. See Hot Car Water Bottles: Does Heat Release More Plastic? for what heat does to PET.
  • Bottled water is usually soft. Brands like Aquafina, Dasani, and Smartwater are purified by reverse osmosis and then re-mineralized lightly. There is almost no calcium carbonate to form scale.
  • The use case is wrong. Boiling is a kitchen ritual. Bottled water is consumed in cars, gyms, planes, and offices — places without a stove.

What boiling does not do

Even in the best-case hard-water scenario, boiling is narrowly useful. It does not handle most of what people worry about in tap water:

  • Chlorine and chloramine: chlorine evaporates with prolonged boiling, but chloramine (used by many US utilities) does not.
  • Lead, copper, arsenic: heavy metals concentrate as water evaporates — boiling makes them worse, not better.
  • PFAS ("forever chemicals"): boiling concentrates them. The EPA explicitly warns against it as a removal method.
  • Pesticides and pharmaceuticals: mostly unaffected.
  • Nanoplastics in soft water: as noted, removal drops to about a quarter.

For a heavy-metal or PFAS problem you want reverse osmosis or activated carbon, not a kettle. The trade-offs are covered in Reverse Osmosis vs Ultrafiltration for Microplastics and Activated Carbon vs. Ultrafiltration: Which Removes More?.

It is not a portable solution, not a complete water-treatment solution, and not relevant to the bottled-water exposure most Americans actually face. When boiling makes sense — and when it doesn't

Boil-and-strain is worth doing if all of the following are true:

  • Your tap water is moderately hard or harder (check your municipal water report or a hardness test strip).
  • You are filling a kettle, carafe, or pitcher at home and not in a hurry.
  • Your main concern is microplastics, not lead or PFAS.
  • You are willing to filter the cooled water through paper or fine mesh.

It is the wrong tool if you are dealing with bottled water at the gym, water from an airport tap, a single-use bottle in the car, or any situation where you are not standing next to a stove. For those moments, a screw-on cap filter that fits a standard DIN 1881 bottle thread does the same job mechanically — through a 0.1-micron membrane — in a few seconds. The bottom line

Boiling tap water is a real, evidence-backed way to cut microplastic intake — but only in a narrow set of conditions: hard water, plus a straining step, plus a kitchen. The 2024 study is being widely misread as a universal hack. It isn't. It is a free trick for people on hard municipal water who drink mostly boiled or filtered tap. For everyone drinking bottled water on the move, the answer is mechanical filtration at the point of drinking — not heat.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does boiling water remove microplastics?

Partially. A 2024 study showed boiling hard tap water can remove up to 90% of nano- and microplastic particles, but only when limescale forms and the cooled water is strained through a fine mesh afterward. In soft water the removal rate drops to about 25%.

How long do you have to boil water to remove microplastics?

The study used a five-minute rolling boil followed by about ten minutes of cooling so the scale flakes could settle. Boiling longer does not improve capture — what matters is letting calcium carbonate crystals form around the particles and then physically filtering them out before drinking.

Can I boil bottled water to remove microplastics?

It is not effective and can make things worse. Most bottled water is softened or purified, so almost no limescale forms. Heating water in a PET bottle also releases additional plastic and antimony, so pouring bottled water into a kettle adds plastic instead of removing it.

Does boiling remove PFAS, lead, or chlorine?

No. Boiling concentrates PFAS, lead, and arsenic because water evaporates while the contaminants stay. Chlorine evaporates but chloramine, used by many US utilities, does not. The EPA explicitly warns against boiling as a treatment for heavy metals or forever chemicals.

Is boiling better than a filter for microplastics?

For someone on hard municipal water at home, boil-and-strain can roughly match a basic filter on microplastic removal. For bottled water, soft tap water, or anything consumed away from a kitchen, a 0.1-micron membrane filter is more reliable and dramatically more convenient.

Why does hard water trap more microplastics when boiled?

Hard water contains dissolved calcium and magnesium bicarbonates. When heated, these turn into solid calcium carbonate crystals — the limescale you see in a kettle. As the crystals form they encapsulate plastic particles, creating flakes large enough to be caught by a coffee filter or mesh.
