Nanoplastics vs Microplastics: Size, Behavior, and Why the Difference Matters

Microplastics are plastic fragments between 1 micrometer and 5 millimeters. Nanoplastics are smaller than 1 micrometer (1,000 nanometers). The size gap matters because nanoplastics can cross biological barriers that stop larger particles.

February 10, 2025 8 min read Sources: EFSA, Columbia, NYU

Kurz: Microplastics and nanoplastics are not the same thing. The size threshold is 1 micrometer (1,000 nanometers). Below that line, particles behave differently: they stay suspended longer, slip through standard filters, and can cross cell membranes that block their bigger siblings. This page explains the definitions, the biology, and why researchers now treat nanoplastics as a separate problem.

The Size Cutoff: Where Microplastic Ends and Nanoplastic Begins

Scientists divide plastic debris into size categories. The most widely used scheme comes from the European Food Safety Authority and matches how most peer-reviewed papers in 2024 and 2025 report their results. Microplastics are fragments larger than 1 micrometer but smaller than 5 millimeters. Nanoplastics fall below 1 micrometer, which is the same as 1,000 nanometers. Some chemists use a stricter cutoff of 100 nanometers for true nanomaterials, but the 1-micrometer line is now standard in environmental health work.

To picture the gap: a human hair is roughly 70 micrometers wide. A red blood cell is about 7 micrometers. A typical bacterium is 1 to 2 micrometers. A virus particle sits around 100 nanometers. Nanoplastics live in that virus-size territory. They are too small to see with a standard light microscope and too small to settle in still water within any practical time frame.

Why the Definition Took So Long to Settle

For years, the term "microplastic" was used as a catch-all. A 2004 paper in Science by Richard Thompson popularized the word, but no upper or lower bound was attached. Researchers reported "microplastics in seafood" without saying whether they meant 4-millimeter shards or sub-micron fragments. Comparing studies was nearly impossible. The 1-micrometer cutoff stuck because it matches the resolution limit of most chemical fingerprinting methods. Anything smaller needs different, more expensive instruments — and that is exactly why nanoplastics went underreported until very recently.

How the Two Behave in Water

Microplastics behave like tiny pieces of trash. They float if they are less dense than water (polyethylene, polypropylene) and sink if they are denser (PET, PVC). In a still glass of water, larger fragments settle within hours or days. Standard 0.2-micron filters catch them reliably. Nanoplastics do not behave that way. They are dominated by Brownian motion — the random jiggling caused by water molecules bumping into them — and electrostatic forces. They stay suspended indefinitely, even in still water. They pass through filters that capture every microplastic. This is the core reason a hub like our microplastics research overview matters: bottled water studies that find "240,000 plastic particles per liter" are mostly counting nanoplastics, not visible flakes.

Practical Consequences

  • Nanoplastics resist gravity-based separation, so settling tanks at water treatment plants miss them.
  • They slip past most household pitcher filters. See Do Brita and Pitcher Filters Remove Microplastics?.
  • They concentrate in biological tissues over time rather than passing through.
  • They are harder to count, which means published exposure numbers almost certainly undercount the smallest fractions.

The Biology: Why Smaller Particles Travel Further

Size determines what a particle can do inside a body. The human gut, the lung, and the blood-brain barrier all have rough size limits for what they let through. A 50-micrometer microplastic flake is far too large to cross intestinal walls under normal conditions. It transits the gut and leaves with stool. A 100-nanometer nanoplastic is a different story. Particles in that range can be taken up by epithelial cells through endocytosis — the same process cells use to import nutrients. Once inside, they can move into lymph, blood, and organs.

The 2024 paper in Toxicological Sciences that found polyethylene fragments in human carotid plaque reported particle sizes well under 1 micrometer. The Microplastics Found in Prostate Cancer Tumors: What the New NYU Study Means from NYU also found sub-micron fragments inside tumor tissue. Across the new wave of human-body studies, the particles being detected are overwhelmingly nanoplastics, not the larger microplastics most people picture.

Surface Area: The Hidden Multiplier

Halve a particle's diameter and you increase its surface-area-to-volume ratio by a factor of two. Shrink a microplastic into a million nanoplastic fragments of the same total mass, and the surface area can grow more than a thousandfold. Surface area matters because plastics adsorb chemicals — bisphenols, phthalates, PFAS, heavy metals — from their surroundings. Smaller particles carry proportionally more of these passengers per gram of plastic. They also release additives like plasticizers faster because more of the polymer is exposed to water.

A microgram of microplastic and a microgram of nanoplastic do not present the same chemical load to a cell. The nanoplastic mass is split across vastly more particles, each interacting with tissue independently.

How Nanoplastics Form

Most nanoplastics are not manufactured. They are weathered. UV light, mechanical friction, heat, and oxidation break larger plastics into smaller and smaller fragments. A single PET bottle exposed to sunlight for months can shed billions of sub-micron fragments. Squeezing a bottle, twisting the cap, and even pouring water generate fresh nanoplastics from the bottle's inner wall. Heat accelerates the process — which is why questions about hot water and Boiling Water and Microplastics: Does Heat Help? are common search topics. The shorter the polymer chain becomes, the more easily it fragments further.

Primary vs Secondary Nanoplastics

  1. Primary nanoplastics are intentionally manufactured at nano-scale — used in some cosmetics, paints, and industrial coatings.
  2. Secondary nanoplastics form from the weathering of larger plastic items. These dominate the environmental burden.

Detection: Why Counting Nanoplastics Is Hard

Microplastics can be filtered onto a membrane and identified one by one using infrared spectroscopy. Nanoplastics are too small for that. Researchers instead use pyrolysis gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (Py-GC/MS), which burns the sample and identifies the polymer fingerprint in the gases released, or stimulated Raman scattering microscopy, the technique behind the 2024 Columbia University bottled-water study. Each method has tradeoffs: Py-GC/MS gives mass but not particle counts; Raman gives counts but is slow and expensive. This is the main reason exposure estimates vary so wildly between studies. For context on those numbers, see How Much Microplastic Do You Eat Per Week? Credit-Card Study Explained.

What This Means for What You Drink

If you only ever cared about visible plastic flakes, a coarse strainer would solve the problem. But the particles that show up in Microplastics in Human Blood: What the 2024-2025 Studies Found studies are not visible. They are nanoplastics — and they are present in nearly every bottled-water sample tested in 2024 and 2025. A filter that targets microplastics down to 0.2 micrometers catches the large majority of particles by count, but it does not catch every true nanoplastic. No portable filter does. The honest framing is that filtration reduces exposure significantly, not that it eliminates plastic from drinking water.

The Bottom Line

Microplastics and nanoplastics share a chemistry, but they are not the same hazard. Microplastics are mostly an exposure-route problem: visible litter, contamination in Microplastics in Food: Salt, Seafood, Tea Bags & Produce, fragments in Microplastics in Tap Water: How They Get In and What You Can Do. Nanoplastics are a biology problem: small enough to cross barriers, numerous enough to coat tissue, and reactive enough to carry other chemicals along for the ride. Treat them as related but distinct. When you read a study, check which size range it actually measured — because a paper that reports "no microplastics detected" may have missed the entire nanoplastic fraction simply because the instruments could not see that small.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the size difference between microplastics and nanoplastics?

Microplastics measure between 1 micrometer and 5 millimeters. Nanoplastics are smaller than 1 micrometer, which equals 1,000 nanometers. The 1-micrometer line is the standard cutoff used in most 2024 and 2025 research.

Are nanoplastics more dangerous than microplastics?

Researchers consider nanoplastics potentially more concerning because they can cross cell membranes and biological barriers that block larger microplastics. They also have far more surface area per gram, which means they can carry more adsorbed chemicals into tissues.

Can water filters remove nanoplastics?

No portable filter removes every nanoplastic. Filters with 0.2-micrometer pores remove the vast majority of particles by count, including all microplastics and many nanoplastics, but the smallest sub-100-nanometer fragments can still pass through.

Where do nanoplastics come from?

Most nanoplastics are secondary, formed when larger plastics break down through UV exposure, friction, heat, and oxidation. A smaller fraction is manufactured at nano-scale for products like cosmetics and industrial coatings.

How do scientists detect nanoplastics?

Standard microscopy cannot see nanoplastics. Researchers use techniques like pyrolysis gas chromatography-mass spectrometry and stimulated Raman scattering microscopy, the method used in the 2024 Columbia bottled-water study that identified hundreds of thousands of particles per liter.

Do bottled water studies measure microplastics or nanoplastics?

Recent high-profile studies measure both, but the dramatic particle counts come almost entirely from the nanoplastic fraction. The 2024 Columbia study found that roughly 90 percent of plastic particles in bottled water samples were nanoplastics rather than microplastics.
