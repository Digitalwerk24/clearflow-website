Hollow Fiber Membrane Filters: The Tech Behind Cap-Style Microplastic Removal

A bundle of microscopic straws with pores 700x smaller than a human hair — here's how it actually traps microplastics

January 15, 2025 8 min read Topic: Ultrafiltration

TL;DR: A hollow fiber membrane filter is a bundle of straw-like fibers with pores around 0.1 microns. Water flows through the walls; microplastics, sediment, and most bacteria stay out. It's the same proven tech used in field filters — now shrunk into a bottle cap.

Kurz: A hollow fiber membrane filter is a bundle of straw-like fibers with pores around 0.1 microns. Water passes through the fiber walls; microplastics, sediment, and most bacteria stay behind. It's the same proven technology backpackers have trusted for decades — and it's what makes a small cap-style filter capable of cleaning the bottled water you already drink.

If you've read our pillar on microplastic filtration technology, you know there are several ways to physically block plastic particles. This page goes one level deeper into the specific membrane format that powers most portable filters: hollow fiber ultrafiltration.

What a Hollow Fiber Membrane Actually Is

Imagine a drinking straw. Now imagine its wall isn't smooth plastic but a microscopic sponge with millions of tiny channels running through it. That's a single hollow fiber. The straw is hollow on the inside, but its wall is porous — and those pores are the filter.

A real filter doesn't contain one fiber. It contains a bundle, often hundreds or thousands of them, packed lengthwise into a small housing. Water is forced against the outside of the bundle. The only way through is sideways through the fiber walls and into the hollow centers, which collect the now-clean water and channel it to the outlet.

The material is usually a polymer — polysulfone (PSU), polyethersulfone (PES), or polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF). These plastics are spun into fibers using a controlled process that locks in pore size during manufacturing. The result is a membrane that doesn't rely on chemicals, electricity, or gravity tricks. It's a purely mechanical barrier. If a particle is bigger than the pore, it doesn't get through. Period.

Why "hollow fiber" beats a flat sheet

A flat membrane sheet only filters across its surface area. A bundle of hollow fibers multiplies that surface area dramatically inside the same volume. A cartridge the size of a thumb can pack 0.1 square meters or more of effective filtration area. That's how a filter small enough to screw onto a standard plastic bottle can still deliver decent flow.

The Physics: Size Exclusion, Not Magnetism

People sometimes assume filters use electrical charge or chemistry to grab particles. Hollow fiber membranes work on a much simpler principle: size exclusion. Each pore is a fixed-diameter opening. Anything larger than that opening physically cannot pass through.

Typical ultrafiltration hollow fibers have pores around 0.01 to 0.1 microns — that's 10 to 100 nanometers. Compare that to what's floating in plastic bottled water:

Particle Typical Size Stopped by 0.1 µm hollow fiber?
Sediment, sand 50–500 µm Yes
Hair, dust 30–100 µm Yes
Bacteria (E. coli, salmonella) 0.5–5 µm Yes
Microplastic fragments (typical) 1–500 µm Yes
Microplastic fibers 10–5,000 µm Yes
Small nanoplastics 0.001–0.1 µm Partial
Dissolved salts, metals < 0.001 µm No
Viruses 0.02–0.3 µm Partial

Microplastics, as defined by NOAA and most researchers, are particles between 1 micron and 5 millimeters. Every single one of them is bigger than a 0.1-micron pore by at least a factor of ten. Hollow fiber ultrafiltration doesn't just block microplastics — it overshoots them. For more on the size question, see our breakdown of filter pore sizes and what they block.

UF vs MF vs NF vs RO: Where Hollow Fiber Sits

Membrane filtration is usually grouped into four categories by pore size. Hollow fiber sits firmly in the ultrafiltration zone, though some microfiltration products use the same fiber format.

  • Microfiltration (MF): 0.1–10 µm pores. Removes sediment, protozoa, most bacteria. Some microplastics escape if smaller.
  • Ultrafiltration (UF): 0.001–0.1 µm pores. Removes everything MF does, plus all microplastics, viruses, and large molecules. This is where most hollow fiber products live.
  • Nanofiltration (NF): 0.001 µm. Removes some dissolved salts. Requires high pressure.
  • Reverse Osmosis (RO): 0.0001 µm. Removes dissolved salts, fluoride, heavy metals. Wastes water; not portable.

If you want to dig into why UF is the sweet spot for portable microplastic removal — and not RO — read our comparison of reverse osmosis vs ultrafiltration.

What hollow fiber does NOT remove

This is where most marketing copy gets dishonest. A hollow fiber membrane is a mechanical sieve. It does not remove anything dissolved in water — chlorine, fluoride, lead, arsenic, pesticides, pharmaceuticals, or taste-and-odor compounds all pass straight through. Those need activated carbon, ion exchange, or RO. Our page on activated carbon vs ultrafiltration covers that trade-off in detail.

How Water Actually Moves Through the Filter

Inside a cap-style filter, the flow path goes like this:

  1. You squeeze or invert the bottle, applying pressure.
  2. Water enters the housing on the outside of the fiber bundle.
  3. Pressure pushes water sideways through the porous fiber walls.
  4. Microplastics and other oversized particles get trapped against the outer fiber surface.
  5. Clean water collects inside the hollow cores.
  6. The cores all drain to a single outlet — the spout you drink from.

The technical term for this is "outside-in" flow. There are also "inside-out" configurations, but outside-in is more common for portable use because trapped particles can often be flushed off the outer surface by reversing flow — a simple form of cap filter maintenance that extends lifespan.

Flow Rate, Pressure, and the Engineering Trade-Off

Smaller pores mean cleaner water. They also mean slower flow. A 0.01-micron membrane catches more, but requires more pressure to push water through. A 0.1-micron membrane flows faster but lets through some viruses (though still 100% of microplastics).

Cap-style filters typically land around 0.1 microns as the sensible compromise: small enough to block every microplastic ever measured in bottled water, large enough to drink at a comfortable rate without industrial-strength pressure. Squeezing a soft PET bottle generates roughly 1–3 PSI — plenty for a well-designed 0.1-micron membrane.

Pore Size Flow Behavior Microplastic Capture
0.2 µm Fast, low effort 100% of microplastics
0.1 µm Moderate, easy squeeze 100% of microplastics + bacteria
0.05 µm Slow, firm squeeze needed 100% + some nanoplastics
0.01 µm Very slow, requires pump 100% + viruses + nanoplastics

Lifespan: Why Filters Eventually Stop

Hollow fiber membranes don't "wear out" the way carbon does. They fail in one of two ways: clogging or fouling. Clogging is when trapped particles physically block enough pores that flow drops below usable. Fouling is when biofilm, oils, or organics coat the outer fiber surface.

For a cap filter used on clean bottled water — which has very low sediment to begin with — the limiting factor is usually time and biological growth, not particle load. That's why we recommend drying the filter between uses and replacing it on schedule rather than chasing a fixed gallon count. The deeper rationale is in our maintenance guide.

How Hollow Fiber Got Into Your Bottle Cap

The technology isn't new. Hollow fiber dialysis membranes have been used in medical settings since the 1960s, and outdoor brands have used the same format in field filters since the 1990s. According to the Wikipedia article on ultrafiltration, municipal water plants have also adopted hollow fiber UF for treating surface water at scale.

What's new is the miniaturization. Improvements in fiber spinning and housing design have made it possible to fit enough effective membrane area into a 30–40 mm threaded cap to handle a 500 ml bottle in 20–30 seconds. That's the engineering shift that made on-the-go microplastic filtration practical — and it's the foundation of every product we make. Hollow Fiber vs Other Microplastic Removal Methods

People often ask whether boiling, distillation, or a regular pitcher filter would work just as well. Short answer: no, and each fails for a different reason.

  • Pitcher filters like Brita rely mostly on activated carbon. Carbon catches some larger fragments but isn't a true membrane — see do Brita filters remove microplastics.
  • Boiling can actually release more particles from the bottle into the water — covered in boiling water and microplastics.
  • Distillation works but isn't portable and is overkill if microplastics are your only concern. See distillation vs filtration.

For the specific job of removing microplastics from bottled water on the move, hollow fiber UF is the right tool — small enough to carry, fast enough to drink from, and physically incapable of letting a microplastic particle pass.

Bottom Line

A hollow fiber membrane filter works because physics works. Pore size is fixed during manufacturing, microplastics are always bigger than the pore, and water flows under everyday pressure. There's nothing magical about it — and nothing fragile either. It's why a piece of technology developed for kidney dialysis can now sit on top of a 16-ounce water bottle and do exactly what it claims: trap microplastics, and only microplastics.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a hollow fiber membrane filter made of?

Most hollow fiber filters are made from food-safe polymers like polysulfone (PSU), polyethersulfone (PES), or PVDF. These materials are spun into thin fibers with a porous wall structure, then bundled inside a housing. The plastic itself is inert and does not shed into the filtered water under normal use.

What's the difference between hollow fiber and a regular water filter?

A regular pitcher filter usually relies on activated carbon granules that adsorb dissolved chemicals like chlorine. A hollow fiber filter is a physical membrane with fixed pores around 0.1 microns. Carbon improves taste, but hollow fiber blocks particles by size — including every microplastic ever measured in bottled water.

Can a hollow fiber filter remove nanoplastics?

It depends on pore size. A 0.1-micron membrane catches all microplastics and the larger end of nanoplastics, but the smallest nanoplastics (under 100 nm) can pass through. A 0.01-micron membrane catches more nanoplastics but requires far more pressure, which is why most portable cap filters land at 0.1 microns.

How long does a hollow fiber filter last?

Lifespan depends on water quality and drying habits. For filtering pre-bottled water with low sediment, a cap-style hollow fiber filter typically lasts several weeks to a few months. Drying it between uses and replacing on schedule prevents biofilm buildup, which is the more common failure mode than physical clogging.

Do hollow fiber filters need electricity or batteries?

No. Hollow fiber filtration is purely mechanical — pressure from squeezing the bottle pushes water through the membrane. There are no electronics, no UV lamps, and no chemicals involved. That's why the technology works for portable, off-grid, and travel use.

Does a hollow fiber filter remove chlorine or lead?

No. Hollow fiber membranes block particles by size, but dissolved substances like chlorine, fluoride, and most heavy metals are far smaller than any pore. Removing those requires activated carbon, ion exchange, or reverse osmosis. A cap-style hollow fiber filter is purpose-built for microplastics, not chemistry.
