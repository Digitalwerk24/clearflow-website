Spring, Purified, and Distilled Bottled Water: What the Labels Really Mean

The three biggest words on a US bottled water label are legally defined — here's what each one actually tells you

February 12, 2025 6 min read Sources: FDA, IBWA, Columbia University

TL;DR: Spring water comes from an underground source. Purified water is tap or spring water cleaned to a strict purity standard. Distilled water is purified by boiling and re-condensing. The labels are legally defined by the FDA — but none of them are required to remove microplastics.

Kurz: The three biggest words on a US bottled water label — spring, purified, and distilled — are not marketing fluff. They are legal categories defined by the FDA. Each one tells you something specific about where the water came from and how it was treated. None of them, however, tell you whether the bottle contains microplastics. This guide decodes the labels and shows you what really matters before you grab a bottle.

If you've ever stood in front of a cooler and wondered why one bottle says "natural spring water" and another says "purified drinking water" — and whether the $1 price gap is real — you're in the right place. This is the companion piece to our hub on Plastic Bottles, Brands & Smarter Drinking Choices, focused entirely on what's written on the label.

How the FDA defines bottled water

In the US, bottled water is regulated by the Food and Drug Administration as a packaged food. The relevant rule is 21 CFR 165.110, which spells out the standard of identity for every common label term. The International Bottled Water Association (IBWA) layers on additional voluntary standards, and the EPA sets the tap-water rules that purified water often starts from.

The rule of thumb: spring describes a source, purified and distilled describe a treatment. That single distinction explains 80% of the label differences you see on the shelf.

Spring water: defined by where it comes from

Per the FDA, "spring water" must be collected from an underground formation where water flows naturally to the surface. It can be captured at the spring itself or through a borehole tapping the same underground source. The composition at the bottling plant must match the natural spring composition.

What this means in practice:

  • Spring water keeps its natural minerals — calcium, magnesium, bicarbonates — which is why it often tastes "rounder" than purified water.
  • It is typically filtered for sediment and disinfected (usually with UV or ozone), but it is not stripped down to pure H₂O.
  • The mineral profile depends entirely on the geology of the source. Two spring waters can taste completely different.

Big US examples include Poland Spring (Maine), Crystal Geyser (multiple sources), and Arrowhead (California, Colorado). For deeper data on what shows up in named brands, see our breakdown of Which Bottled Water Brands Have the Most Microplastics?.

Purified water: defined by the treatment process

"Purified water" is the workhorse category. The FDA borrows the definition from the US Pharmacopeia: the water must contain no more than 10 parts per million of total dissolved solids (TDS). To hit that target, manufacturers use one or more of:

  • Reverse osmosis (RO) — pushes water through a semipermeable membrane that rejects 95–99% of dissolved solids.
  • Distillation — boils water into steam, then condenses it back into liquid.
  • Deionization — uses ion-exchange resins to strip charged particles.
  • Carbon filtration plus polishing — to remove chlorine, taste, and organics.

Crucially, the source water can be anything that meets EPA drinking-water standards — including municipal tap. Aquafina (PepsiCo) and Dasani (Coca-Cola) are the two best-known examples; both start with public water systems and run it through multi-stage purification.

If you want a deeper look at how those treatment steps compare for microplastic-sized particles, our guide on Distillation vs Filtration for Microplastic Removal walks through it step by step.

Distilled water: the purest of the three

Distilled water is a subset of purified water with one specific method: boil, capture the steam, condense it back into a liquid. Almost everything heavier than water — minerals, salts, most bacteria, many chemical residues — gets left behind in the boiler.

The result is essentially pure H₂O. That's great for:

  • CPAP machines and humidifiers (no mineral scale)
  • Steam irons
  • Car batteries
  • Lab use

It's less popular as a daily drinking water because it tastes flat — minerals are what give water flavor — and because it provides no dietary mineral contribution. Distilled water is safe to drink, but most people don't enjoy it.

Which one has the most microplastics?

This is the question that should actually drive your decision — and the labels won't help you answer it. The 2018 Orb Media / SUNY Fredonia study tested 259 bottles across 11 brands in 9 countries and found microplastic particles in 93% of them, across spring, purified, and mineral categories. The 2024 Columbia University nanoplastics study went further: it detected roughly 240,000 plastic fragments per liter in three major US bottled brands, the vast majority of them nanoplastic-sized.

Two takeaways from the research:

  1. The bottle itself contributes plastic — PET sheds particles, and caps shed even more when twisted. That happens regardless of whether the inside is "spring" or "purified."
  2. Purified water is treated more aggressively than spring water, but the bottling line is the same. Particles enter at filling, capping, and during storage.

For the full evidence summary, see Microplastics in Bottled Water: What the Studies Actually Show. If you're comparing bottled to tap, Microplastics in Bottled Water vs Tap Water: Which Has More? has the head-to-head numbers.

So which should you actually buy?

It depends on what you want from the water:

  • You want taste and minerals → spring water, ideally from a single named source.
  • You want consistency and the lowest TDS → purified water.
  • You need pure H₂O for a device or recipe → distilled water.
  • You want fewer microplastics → none of the three labels guarantee that. The label tells you about minerals and source, not about plastic shedding.

The smarter move is to pick the water you like the taste of and then deal with the bottle separately. Quick label red flags to ignore

Some terms on bottled water are pure marketing and have no FDA definition:

  • "Premium" — no legal meaning.
  • "Glacier" — only meaningful if paired with "spring" or a verifiable source.
  • "Mountain" — descriptive, not regulated.
  • "Alkaline" — refers to pH, often added electrolytes; no purity guarantee.
  • "Pure" — unless followed by "purified" with the FDA definition, it's branding.

Stick to the four legally defined terms: spring, purified, distilled, mineral, plus artesian. Everything else is decoration.

Bottom line

Spring, purified, and distilled aren't ranked from worst to best — they describe three different things. Spring tells you where the water came from. Purified tells you how aggressively it was cleaned. Distilled tells you a specific cleaning method that produces near-pure H₂O. Once you know the labels, you can pick on taste, price, and intended use. Frequently Asked Questions

Is spring water healthier than purified water?

Not in any clinically meaningful way. Spring water contains naturally occurring minerals like calcium and magnesium, which give it taste, but the amounts are small compared to what you get from food. Purified water has those minerals stripped out but is held to a tighter purity standard. For most healthy adults, the choice is about taste and price, not health.

Can you drink distilled water every day?

Yes, it is safe. The common claim that distilled water leaches minerals from your body is not supported by evidence for people eating a normal diet. The bigger reason most people don't drink it daily is that it tastes flat because there are no minerals to give it flavor.

Is Aquafina spring water?

No. Aquafina is purified water that starts from municipal tap sources and is treated with reverse osmosis and other steps to meet the FDA purified standard. The same is true of Dasani. Brands like Poland Spring, Arrowhead, and Crystal Geyser are actual spring water.

Does purified water have microplastics?

Yes, studies consistently find microplastic and nanoplastic particles in purified bottled water. The purification step removes dissolved solids but the particles enter from the PET bottle, the cap, and the bottling line itself. The label term does not predict microplastic content.

What is the difference between purified and distilled water?

Distilled is a specific method (boil and condense) that produces purified water. Purified is the broader category — it can be made by distillation, reverse osmosis, deionization, or a combination. All distilled water is purified, but not all purified water is distilled.

Why does spring water taste better?

Minerals like calcium, magnesium, and bicarbonates dissolved in the water carry flavor. Spring water retains its source minerals, which give it a rounder, slightly sweet taste. Purified and especially distilled water have those minerals removed, which is why they taste neutral or flat to most people.
