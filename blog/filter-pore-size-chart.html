Filter Pore Size Chart: What Each Micron Rating Actually Blocks

A clear water filter pore size chart comparing microns and nanometers against sediment, microplastics, bacteria, viruses, and nanoplastics

January 15, 2025 8 min read Reference Guide

TL;DR: Filter pore size tells you what gets through and what gets trapped. Sediment filters stop at 5 microns, microfiltration at 0.1, ultrafiltration at 0.01, and reverse osmosis at 0.0001. Microplastics start around 1 micron — most cap and hollow-fiber filters handle them easily.

Microplastics start around 1 micron and run down to roughly 1 micron at the low end of "micro" — so most well-rated hollow-fiber and cap-style filters block them easily.

Why filter pore size is the number that matters

Every water filter has a rated pore size. That number — usually written in microns (µm) or nanometers (nm) — describes the largest particle the filter will let through under normal operating pressure. Everything bigger gets trapped on the membrane or inside a depth media. Everything smaller passes.

Pore size is the single most useful spec on a filter label because it sets a hard physical limit. A filter rated at 0.1 micron cannot remove a 0.001-micron dissolved salt ion, no matter how slowly you push water through it. Conversely, a 0.1-micron filter will reliably remove a 1-micron microplastic fragment because the particle simply does not fit. This is called size-exclusion filtration, and it is the dominant mechanism for physical water filters.

Two filters with the same pore-size number can still behave differently. "Nominal" ratings mean the filter blocks ~85–95% of particles at that size. "Absolute" ratings mean it blocks 99.9%+. Always check which one a manufacturer uses.

Microns and nanometers: getting the units straight

The two units used in filtration are micrometers (microns, µm) and nanometers (nm). They differ by a factor of 1,000.

  • 1 millimeter = 1,000 microns
  • 1 micron = 1,000 nanometers
  • 1 nanometer = 0.001 micron

So a virus listed as 30 nm is the same as 0.03 micron. A 0.2-micron filter is the same as a 200-nm filter. When you compare specs across brands, convert everything to one unit first — most marketing material mixes them.

The filter pore size chart

Here are the standard filtration categories, the typical pore sizes, and what they target.

Category Pore Size (µm) Pore Size (nm) Primary Targets
Coarse / sediment 50–100 50,000–100,000 Sand, rust flakes, hair
Sediment (fine) 1–10 1,000–10,000 Silt, fine grit, large microplastics
Microfiltration (MF) 0.1–1 100–1,000 Bacteria, cysts, most microplastics
Ultrafiltration (UF) 0.01–0.1 10–100 Viruses, colloids, large nanoplastics
Nanofiltration (NF) 0.001–0.01 1–10 Dissolved organics, hardness ions
Reverse osmosis (RO) 0.0001–0.001 0.1–1 Salts, fluoride, smallest nanoplastics

The pattern is logarithmic. Each step down doesn't just "refine" the previous one — it removes an entirely new class of contaminant. Sediment filters protect equipment from clogging. Microfiltration removes most of what you can't see but might still feel (cloudy water, mild taste). Ultrafiltration crosses into pathogen territory. Reverse osmosis strips water down to nearly pure H₂O.

Contaminant size chart: what's actually in water

Now flip the question. Instead of "what does this filter block," ask "how big is the thing I want gone?" These are typical size ranges measured in real water samples.

Contaminant Typical Size Smallest Filter Needed
Sand grains 100–2,000 µm Coarse sediment
Human hair 50–100 µm Coarse sediment
Silt and clay 1–50 µm Sediment (fine)
Giardia cysts 8–14 µm 1 µm absolute
Microplastic fibers 10–500 µm length 1 µm or finer
Microplastic fragments 1–100 µm 0.5–1 µm
Bacteria (E. coli) 0.5–5 µm 0.2 µm absolute
Smallest microplastics 1–5 µm 0.2–0.5 µm
Nanoplastics (large) 100–1,000 nm (0.1–1 µm) 0.1 µm or UF
Viruses 20–300 nm Ultrafiltration
Nanoplastics (small) 1–100 nm UF / RO
Dissolved salts, fluoride <1 nm Reverse osmosis

By definition, microplastics are particles smaller than 5 mm and larger than 1 µm. Anything below 1 µm gets reclassified as a nanoplastic. For the full breakdown of where one ends and the other begins, see nanoplastics vs microplastics.

The 0.2-micron benchmark and why it matters

0.2 micron is the de facto standard for portable and cap-style filters, and it is not an arbitrary number. It is the pore size that blocks all common waterborne bacteria — including E. coli, Salmonella, and Cholera — while still allowing a reasonable flow rate. Hollow-fiber membranes built at 0.2 µm or finer are the workhorse of modern travel filtration.

At 0.2 µm, a filter also captures essentially every microplastic particle, because the smallest documented microplastics in drinking water are roughly 1 µm — five times larger than the pore. There is a separate question about nanoplastics smaller than 100 nm, which a 0.2-micron filter will not catch reliably. We cover the trade-offs in 0.2-micron filtration explained.

How filter type maps to pore size

Pore size and physical filter design are linked. You can't make a 0.0001-micron filter out of pleated paper.

  1. Mesh and pleated paper — usually 5 µm and up. Cheap, high flow, low protection.
  2. Granular activated carbon — not a true size rating; works by adsorption, not sieving. Removes taste, chlorine, some organics, but lets microplastics through.
  3. Carbon block — typically 0.5–5 µm. Combines adsorption with mechanical filtration. Stops large microplastics.
  4. Hollow-fiber membrane — 0.01–0.2 µm. The technology used in most portable and cap filters. See hollow fiber membrane filters.
  5. Reverse osmosis — 0.0001 µm. Removes salts and the smallest nanoplastics, but needs pressure and wastes water.

Choosing between these technologies is really a question of what you want gone and how much flow rate you can sacrifice. The deep comparison is in reverse osmosis vs ultrafiltration.

Where microplastics actually sit on the chart

Research published since 2018 has measured microplastic sizes in bottled water and tap water. The distribution is heavily skewed toward larger particles. About 90% of the microplastics found in bottled water samples are above 5 µm. Roughly 5–8% are between 1 and 5 µm. The remainder — under 1 µm — are technically nanoplastics, and they require more sensitive detection methods like Raman microscopy.

For someone choosing a filter today, this means a 0.1 to 0.5-micron filter removes the overwhelming majority of plastic particles people consume. A 0.2-micron portable water filter handles the practical exposure pathway from drinking bottled water, even if it cannot guarantee zero nanoplastic passage.

Limitations of pore-size ratings

Pore size is necessary but not sufficient. Three caveats matter:

  • Nominal vs absolute. A 1-µm nominal filter may let 10% of 1-µm particles through. A 1-µm absolute filter blocks 99.9%+. Same number, very different real-world performance.
  • Flow rate and pressure. Force water through a filter too hard and particles deform or squeeze through. Cap-style filters use gravity or gentle suction, which keeps performance honest.
  • Channeling and damage. A torn or kinked hollow fiber can short-circuit the entire membrane. This is why integrity matters more than the spec sheet. Follow the maintenance routine in our cap filter product guide.

Using the chart to choose a filter

Three quick decisions cover most use cases:

  1. City tap water in the US. Already disinfected. You mainly care about taste, chlorine, and microplastics. A 0.1–0.5 µm filter with carbon is enough.
  2. Bottled water on the go. The water is sealed and microbiologically safe. The only meaningful target is microplastics, so a 0.1–0.2 µm membrane is the right tool.
  3. Backcountry or international travel. Now you also need pathogen protection. Stick to 0.2 µm absolute or finer hollow-fiber filters, ideally with a virus claim (UF-class).

Bottom line: the pore size chart is a map, not a verdict. Match the pore size to the smallest contaminant you actually need to remove, check whether the rating is nominal or absolute, and don't pay for nanofiltration if your only concern is microplastics in bottled water.

Frequently Asked Questions

What micron filter removes microplastics?

A filter rated at 1 micron or smaller removes the vast majority of microplastics found in bottled and tap water. For high confidence, choose 0.1 to 0.5 micron, which catches even the smallest microplastic fragments down to around 1 micron in diameter.

Is 0.2 micron smaller than 0.5 micron?

Yes. 0.2 micron is finer, meaning it blocks smaller particles. A 0.2-micron filter will stop bacteria and microplastics, while a 0.5-micron filter still catches most microplastics but lets some bacteria through.

How small is a nanometer compared to a micron?

One micron equals 1,000 nanometers. So a 100-nanometer particle is 0.1 micron, and a 0.2-micron filter is the same as a 200-nanometer filter.

Can any filter remove nanoplastics?

Reverse osmosis and tight ultrafiltration membranes can remove most nanoplastics down to around 1 nanometer. Standard 0.2-micron filters will not reliably catch nanoplastics under 100 nanometers.

What is the difference between nominal and absolute micron rating?

Nominal means the filter removes roughly 85 to 95 percent of particles at the stated size. Absolute means it removes 99.9 percent or more. Always check which standard a manufacturer is using before comparing two filters with the same micron number.
