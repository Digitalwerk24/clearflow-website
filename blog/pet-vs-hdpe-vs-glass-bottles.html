PET, HDPE, and Glass Bottles: A Plain-English Safety Comparison

Microplastic shedding, heat tolerance, and reuse — how the three most common bottle materials actually compare

February 10, 2025 8 min read Sources: FDA, WHO, Columbia/Rutgers

TL;DR: Glass sheds the fewest microplastics but is heavy and breakable. HDPE (#2) is sturdier and leaches less than PET under heat. PET (#1) is the most common bottled-water plastic and the biggest microplastic source — especially when reused, squeezed, or left warm.

Kurz: If you only care about microplastics, glass wins, HDPE is the better plastic, and PET is the everyday default that needs the most care. This guide is part of our hub on plastic bottles and smarter drinking choices — read on for how the three materials actually behave in real life.

The three bottles you actually encounter

Walk into any U.S. grocery store and you will see three materials dominating the shelves: PET, HDPE, and glass. They look similar at a glance, but they shed different amounts of plastic into your water, handle heat differently, and have very different recycling stories.

PET (polyethylene terephthalate, resin code #1) is the clear, lightweight plastic used for almost every single-serve water bottle in America — Dasani, Aquafina, Poland Spring, Fiji, Evian. HDPE (high-density polyethylene, resin code #2) is the slightly cloudy, stiffer plastic used for milk jugs, larger water jugs, and some sports bottles. Glass is glass: heavy, inert, breakable, and still the gold standard in food-grade packaging.

Every one of these will introduce some microplastics or contaminants into your drink — yes, even glass, because the cap and seal are usually plastic. The differences are in how much and under what conditions.

Quick comparison at a glance

Factor PET (#1) HDPE (#2) Glass
Microplastic shedding Highest Moderate Lowest (cap excluded)

PET (#1): the everyday default

PET is the plastic in your hand 95% of the time you buy bottled water. It is clear, lightweight, shatter-proof, and cheap to produce. It is also the material implicated in nearly every viral microplastic study of bottled water, including the 2024 Columbia/Rutgers study that found an average of 240,000 plastic fragments per liter of bottled water — most of them nanoplastics shed from the bottle itself.

PET sheds particles three ways: from the manufacturing process, from mechanical stress (squeezing, dropping, shaking), and from heat. The softening point is around 158°F, which any car interior in a Texas July easily exceeds. Once PET warms up, it releases antimony and acetaldehyde in addition to plastic fragments — we cover this in detail in our piece on hot-car water bottles.

PET is technically food-safe for single use. The FDA classifies it as Generally Recognized as Safe, and you can read the FDA's official PET guidance for the regulatory position. But "safe" here means "will not poison you in a single serving," not "will not shed plastic." Those are different questions.

When PET goes wrong

  • Left in a hot car or sunny window
  • Refilled and squeezed daily for weeks
  • Frozen and thawed repeatedly
  • Used past its printed shelf life — see our shelf-life guide
  • Filled with carbonated, acidic, or hot liquid

HDPE (#2): the underrated middle option

HDPE is the cloudy plastic used for gallon water jugs, milk, and many reusable sports bottles. It is stiffer than PET, handles heat much better (softens around 248°F), and is less prone to surface scratching that releases particles.

HDPE still sheds microplastics — every plastic does — but the numbers are typically lower than PET in comparable studies, because the polymer is denser and less reactive with water. It is also one of the few plastics considered acceptable for limited reuse. If you are deciding between buying a single gallon HDPE jug or twelve PET half-liter bottles, the HDPE jug exposes you to less plastic per ounce of water consumed.

The trade-offs: HDPE is opaque, less premium-looking, and rarely used for the small grab-and-go format consumers actually want. You almost never see it in convenience-store coolers.

Glass: the cleanest option with real-world friction

Glass is chemically inert. It does not leach. It does not shed microplastics from the body of the bottle. Studies that compare glass-bottled water to plastic-bottled water consistently find the glass version has fewer particles — though not zero, because nearly every glass bottle uses a plastic-lined metal cap or a full plastic closure, and that closure is in direct contact with the water.

The catch is everything that is not the chemistry. Glass weighs five to ten times more than PET, breaks when dropped, and roughly doubles the carbon footprint per unit shipped. Brands like Voss and Mountain Valley sell premium glass at premium prices precisely because the logistics are punishing.

For at-home consumption, glass is a sensible upgrade. For the gym, the airport, or a hike, glass loses on practicality every time. That tension is why most consumers stay with PET and just try to handle it better — which is exactly the gap our Clear Flow cap filter was designed to close.

Microplastic shedding: the numbers that matter

Independent lab work has consistently ranked the three materials in the same order. A representative summary of reported particle counts per liter:

Container Reported microplastic particles/L
Glass-bottled water (with plastic cap liner) ~50–200
HDPE jug water ~300–1,000
PET single-serve bottle ~325 (WHO/Mason 2018) to 240,000 (Columbia 2024, nano-included)
Tap water (US average) ~5–10

The 240,000 figure for PET is not a typo and not an outlier — it is what happens once you start counting nanoplastics smaller than one micron, which older instruments could not detect. We unpack that study in our bottled water microplastics review.

Heat, sunlight, and reuse

Material safety is not just about what is in your hand — it is about what has happened to that bottle before you opened it. A PET bottle that sat in a 130°F warehouse for two weeks is not the same product that left the bottling line. The same bottle handled gently at room temperature is closer to its lab-tested baseline.

  1. Heat: PET begins releasing measurable extra particles above 95°F. HDPE is stable to roughly twice that. Glass is unaffected by any temperature you can produce in a car or kitchen.
  2. UV light: Sunlight breaks down PET surface layers. Bottles stored in clear cases on truck beds are the worst case.
  3. Mechanical stress: Squeezing, biting the cap, dropping, and freezing all create stress fractures that shed particles.
  4. Refilling: See our deep dive on how many times it is okay to refill a plastic bottle. PET fares poorly past 2–3 reuses; HDPE fares better.

Recyclability in the real world

All three materials are technically recyclable. None of them are recycled at the rate consumers assume. The U.S. PET recycling rate has hovered around 29% for a decade; HDPE is similar; glass is around 31% nationally but varies wildly by region. The remaining 70% goes to landfill, incineration, or the environment, where it slowly fragments into the microplastics we drink in the first place.

Glass is infinitely recyclable without quality loss. PET and HDPE downcycle — recycled PET typically becomes carpet fiber or polyester clothing, not new food-grade bottles. The closed loop people imagine when they put a bottle in the blue bin is mostly aspirational.

BPA and "BPA-free" — a side note

None of the three bottle types we are discussing contain BPA in the first place. BPA is a polycarbonate (#7) concern, used in some hard reusable water bottles and food can linings — not in PET, HDPE, or glass. "BPA-free" labeling on a PET bottle is technically true but marketing-driven. What matters more is whether the replacement chemicals (BPS, BPF) are safer, which we cover in our BPA-free bottle analysis.

Which should you actually choose?

There is no single right answer, but here is how we would frame it:

  • If you drink bottled water at home: buy glass when you can, or buy gallon HDPE jugs and decant.
  • If you drink on the go: PET is what is available; the goal becomes minimizing shedding (cool storage, no reuse, no squeezing) and removing what is already there at the point of drinking.
  • If you care about a single metric: microplastic exposure per dollar — HDPE gallon jugs win.
  • If you want zero plastic contact: tap water through a certified filter into a stainless or glass vessel. Compare options in our portable filter picks for 2026.

For most Americans, the realistic answer is: PET is going to stay in your life, so manage it. Keep bottles out of hot cars, don't reuse them indefinitely, and consider adding a cap-style microplastic filter so the bottle you already bought delivers cleaner water.

Bottom line

Glass is the safest material, HDPE is the safest plastic, and PET is the practical default that earns its bad reputation only when it gets abused by heat, sunlight, or repeat use. The bottle on the shelf is rarely the problem on day one — what happens to it before you drink it usually is. Pick the material that fits your lifestyle, then control the conditions you can.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is PET plastic safe to drink from?

PET is FDA-approved as Generally Recognized as Safe for single-use beverage packaging. That means it will not cause acute harm, but it does shed microplastic and nanoplastic particles into water, especially when warmed, squeezed, or reused. For daily exposure minimization, treat PET as a one-time-use container.

Is HDPE safer than PET?

For most everyday purposes, yes. HDPE is denser, more heat-tolerant, and sheds fewer particles per liter than PET under comparable conditions. It also tolerates limited reuse better. The downside is that HDPE bottles are usually opaque and rarely sold in the small single-serve format consumers prefer.

Does glass-bottled water have microplastics?

Yes, but far fewer than plastic-bottled water. The glass itself contributes none, but most caps include a plastic liner in direct contact with the water. Glass-bottled water typically tests in the range of 50 to 200 microplastic particles per liter, versus several hundred to tens of thousands for PET.

Which plastic number is safest for bottled water?

Among bottle plastics, #2 HDPE is generally considered the safest because it is chemically stable, heat-tolerant, and sheds fewer particles. #1 PET is acceptable for single use. Avoid #3 PVC, #6 polystyrene, and #7 (other) for drinking water, especially if the bottle has been heated.

Can I tell what type of plastic my bottle is?

Yes. Flip the bottle over and look at the recycling triangle on the bottom. The number inside identifies the resin: 1 is PET, 2 is HDPE, 5 is polypropylene (common in caps), and 7 is the catch-all for everything else. Almost every clear water bottle sold in the U.S. is #1 PET.
