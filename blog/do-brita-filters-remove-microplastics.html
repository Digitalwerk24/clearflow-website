Do Brita and Pitcher Filters Actually Remove Microplastics?

An honest look at what carafe filters are certified for, what testing actually shows, and where they fall short on plastic particles

January 15, 2026 8 min read Sources: NSF, EPA, peer-reviewed lab studies

TL;DR: Brita and most pitcher filters are not certified to remove microplastics. They use activated carbon designed for chlorine, taste, and some metals. Independent tests suggest partial reduction of larger plastic particles, but nothing close to the performance of a true 0.2-micron membrane.

Kurz: Brita pitchers, PUR, ZeroWater and similar carafe-style filters were built to fix chlorine taste and reduce specific metals — not microplastics. None of the mainstream pitcher brands carry an NSF certification for microplastic removal as of 2026. Some lab tests show they trap a fraction of larger plastic fragments, but the pore structure of granular activated carbon simply isn't built for sub-micron particles.

If you searched "do Brita filters remove microplastics," you probably already suspect the answer is complicated. It is. This page walks through what pitcher filters are actually certified to do, what independent researchers have measured, and where the format genuinely falls short on plastic particles. For the deeper science behind every method, see our pillar on Microplastic Filtration Technology: How Filters Actually Work.

The short answer: not officially, and not reliably

Brita has never claimed to remove microplastics on its packaging, in its product specs, or in any NSF/ANSI certification we could find. The same holds for PUR, ZeroWater, Aquasana pitchers, and most other US carafe filters. Their certifications focus on chlorine, lead (in specific models), mercury, cadmium, copper, zinc, benzene, and pharmaceuticals — meaningful contaminants, but a different category than plastic particles.

NSF/ANSI 42 covers aesthetic claims (chlorine, taste, odor). NSF/ANSI 53 covers health contaminants like lead and VOCs. NSF/ANSI 401 covers emerging contaminants — pesticides, drug residues. There is currently no NSF standard specifically for microplastics in residential pitchers, though NSF released a protocol (NSF P477) used mainly by industrial and commercial filter testing. No Brita pitcher carries it.

How pitcher filters are actually built

Open a Brita Standard cartridge and you'll find two things: granular activated carbon (GAC) and ion-exchange resin beads. The carbon adsorbs chlorine and organic compounds. The resin swaps unwanted metal ions for sodium or hydrogen ions. Neither layer is a true filter in the mechanical-sieve sense. Water flows around grains, not through a defined pore.

Brita's Longlast+ uses a denser pleated media and adds activated carbon for more lead reduction. ZeroWater stacks five stages of resin and carbon. PUR uses a carbon block in some models, which has a tighter structure than loose GAC. But even the tightest carbon blocks in pitcher format typically have nominal pore sizes between 0.5 and 5 microns — and "nominal" means most particles of that size are captured, not all.

Microplastics span from roughly 5 millimeters down to 1 micron. Nanoplastics go smaller still — down to a few nanometers. A filter rated for 5 microns can mechanically intercept the largest fragments, but lets virtually everything below that pass through. What independent testing has found

A handful of academic and consumer-press tests have measured pitcher performance against plastic particles. The results are scattered but reasonably consistent in shape:

Test source Filter type Reduction range Particle size tested
University lab studies (2020-2023) GAC pitcher 40-70% Mostly >10 µm fragments
Consumer-press teardown Carbon block pitcher 60-85% 5-100 µm fragments
Manufacturer self-test (not certified) Multi-stage pitcher Up to 99% Unspecified particle range
Independent ICR-MS analysis Standard pitcher <30% Sub-micron particles

Two patterns stand out. First, performance drops sharply as particle size shrinks. The same pitcher that captures most 20-micron fragments may pass nearly all 1-micron particles. Second, results vary cartridge to cartridge — channeling (water finding the path of least resistance through the carbon bed) is a known issue, and a tired cartridge performs worse than a fresh one.

Why carbon isn't designed for plastic particles

Activated carbon works through adsorption: organic molecules stick to the porous carbon surface through weak chemical attraction. Chlorine, pesticide residues, taste compounds — these all adsorb well.

Microplastics are different. They're solid particles, not dissolved molecules. They don't adsorb the way chlorine does; they have to be physically blocked. That requires a true mechanical filter with a defined pore size, not a packed bed of carbon grains. This is the same reason Activated Carbon vs. Ultrafiltration: Which Removes More? consistently shows ultrafiltration winning on particle removal.

What actually does remove microplastics

If your goal is microplastic reduction specifically, three formats are built for it:

  1. Hollow fiber ultrafiltration — pore sizes around 0.1 to 0.2 microns. Mechanically blocks essentially all microplastics and most bacteria. This is the technology behind most modern portable filters, including cap-style filters that screw onto bottles. See Hollow Fiber Membrane Filters: How They Trap Microplastics.
  2. Reverse osmosis — pore sizes around 0.0001 microns. Removes microplastics, nanoplastics, dissolved salts, and most contaminants. Slow, wastes water, and overkill if microplastics are the only concern.
  3. Distillation — boils water, captures the vapor. Leaves plastic particles behind in the boiling chamber. Effective but energy-intensive and inconvenient.

Pitchers don't use any of these. They were never meant to.

The pitcher vs cap-filter comparison

For households worried about microplastics in bottled or tap water, the practical question is what tool fits the use case. Pitchers are sit-on-counter devices for chlorine and taste. Cap-style filters address the bottled-water problem specifically: you twist a 0.2-micron membrane onto a standard plastic bottle and drink filtered water on the spot.

Neither replaces the other. A pitcher fixes the taste of municipal tap water at home. A cap filter fixes the plastic-particle issue in the bottled water you drink in the car, at the gym, on flights, and during travel. What Brita itself says

Brita's official position, repeated in customer service responses and on its support pages, is that its filters are "not certified or tested to remove microplastics." That's a defensible answer — it's accurate, and it doesn't promise something the cartridge wasn't engineered to do.

The brand has occasionally pointed to its NSF 401 certification, which covers some emerging contaminants. NSF 401 does not include microplastics. The two should not be conflated, even though both fall under the umbrella of "new contaminants of concern."

If you already own a Brita, do this

You don't have to throw it out. A pitcher still earns its place for taste, chlorine, and (in lead-rated models) metal reduction. To get the most from it on the plastic-particle question:

  • Replace cartridges on schedule. A spent cartridge channels worse and traps less of anything.
  • Avoid pouring already-bottled water through it. The microplastic load in bottled water is heavier than tap, and the pitcher won't catch most of it anyway. For why bottled water is the bigger plastic-particle source, see Microplastics in Bottled Water: What the Studies Actually Show.
  • For tap water specifically, the picture is different. Tap water generally has lower microplastic counts than bottled — see Microplastics in Bottled Water vs Tap Water: Which Has More?.
  • If you want plastic-particle removal too, pair the pitcher with a 0.2-micron device for the bottles you drink away from home. Our Clear Flow product guide explains how a cap-style filter fits into that gap.

Bottom line

Brita and other pitcher filters are good at what they were built for — and microplastics weren't on the design brief. Independent testing shows partial reduction of larger fragments, but nothing approaching the performance of a true ultrafiltration membrane. If microplastics are the contaminant that worries you, choose a filter built around a defined sub-micron pore size. For context on the broader debate, the EPA's microplastics research page and Microplastics in Tap Water: How They Get In and What You Can Do are good starting points.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do Brita filters remove microplastics?

Brita filters are not certified to remove microplastics. They use granular activated carbon and ion-exchange resin designed for chlorine, taste, and certain metals. Independent tests show partial reduction of larger plastic fragments but limited performance on smaller particles.

What pore size do Brita pitcher filters have?

Brita pitchers use granular activated carbon, which has no defined pore size in the mechanical-sieve sense. Carbon-block pitchers from competitors range from roughly 0.5 to 5 microns nominal. Most microplastics, especially particles below 5 microns, pass through.

Is ZeroWater better than Brita for microplastics?

ZeroWater's five-stage cartridge captures more dissolved solids than a standard Brita, but it is also not certified for microplastic removal. Some user tests suggest higher particle capture due to denser media, though performance varies and is not independently verified for plastic particles.

Which filter type actually removes microplastics?

Hollow fiber ultrafiltration membranes around 0.1 to 0.2 microns mechanically block essentially all microplastics. Reverse osmosis and distillation also remove them. These technologies are used in portable cap-style filters, under-sink RO systems, and countertop distillers — not in standard carafe pitchers.

Should I stop using my Brita?

No. A Brita still does its intended job well: chlorine reduction, taste improvement, and metal reduction in certified models. If you want microplastic removal too, pair it with a device built for sub-micron particle filtration rather than expecting the pitcher to do both jobs.

Does a PUR filter remove microplastics?

PUR pitchers and faucet attachments are not certified for microplastic removal either. Their carbon-block media may catch larger fragments mechanically, but PUR makes no marketing claim about microplastics and holds no NSF certification covering them.
