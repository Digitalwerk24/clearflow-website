How Microplastics Enter the Human Body

The three main exposure routes — ingestion, inhalation, dermal — and which one really drives your daily intake

February 10, 2025 8 min read Sources: PNAS, NEJM, University of Victoria

TL;DR: Microplastics enter the body through three routes: ingestion (food and especially bottled water), inhalation (indoor dust and synthetic fibers), and minor dermal contact. Drinking is the single largest contributor for most adults — which is also the easiest route to reduce.

Kurz: Microplastics reach your body through three main doors — what you swallow, what you breathe, and what touches your skin. Ingestion is the biggest one, with bottled water alone contributing tens of thousands of particles per year. Inhalation runs a close second in poorly ventilated indoor spaces. Dermal absorption stays minor for intact skin.

If you want the broader picture of where these particles come from and what the research shows, start with our research hub on Microplastics Science: Sources, Exposure & Latest Research. This page focuses on the mechanism question: how, exactly, do microplastics get inside a human body?

The three exposure routes at a glance

Researchers group microplastic exposure into three pathways. The relative contribution depends on your diet, your home, your job, and how much bottled water you drink. The table below shows a typical adult in the United States, based on peer-reviewed estimates collected by the Wikipedia overview of microplastics and recent review papers.

Route Share of daily load Main sources
Ingestion ~60-80% Bottled water, tap water, salt, seafood, tea bags, packaged food
Inhalation ~15-35% Indoor dust, synthetic textile fibers, tire wear, traffic
Dermal <1-2% Cosmetics, microbeads, contact with plastic surfaces

Numbers vary widely between studies because methods are not yet standardized. Still, every major review since 2022 puts ingestion firmly in first place.

Route 1: Ingestion — the biggest door

Most microplastics enter the body through the mouth. They piggyback on the food and drinks we consume every day. Once swallowed, particles travel through the gastrointestinal tract. The largest ones pass through and are excreted. Smaller fragments, especially those below 10 micrometers, can cross the gut lining and reach the lymphatic system and bloodstream.

Bottled water is the single biggest contributor

A 2024 Columbia and Rutgers study using stimulated Raman spectroscopy counted an average of 240,000 plastic particles per liter of bottled water — about 90% of them nanoplastics. For a person drinking two bottles a day, that single habit dwarfs every other ingestion source. We break down the data in our deep dive on Microplastics in Bottled Water: What the Studies Actually Show and on which brands score worst in Which Bottled Water Brands Have the Most Microplastics?.

Tap water, salt and seafood add up

Tap water also contains microplastics, though usually at much lower concentrations than bottled water (see Microplastics in Tap Water: How They Get In and What You Can Do). Sea salt, mussels, shrimp and certain teabags release additional particles, particularly when steeped in hot water. The complete picture of dietary sources is mapped in Microplastics in Food: Salt, Seafood, Tea Bags & Produce.

How much that adds up to per week

The widely cited "credit card a week" figure — around 5 grams of plastic — comes from a 2019 WWF-commissioned analysis. Newer counts using better detection methods land higher. The math behind that estimate and what it actually means is laid out in How Much Microplastic Do You Eat Per Week? Credit-Card Study Explained.

Route 2: Inhalation — the underrated door

Indoor air is dustier than most people realize, and a meaningful share of that dust is plastic. Synthetic carpets shed polyester. Fleece jackets release fibers with every movement. Tires abrade into the air outside, and that traffic-related dust drifts indoors through windows and on shoes.

What gets inhaled and where it goes

Inhaled microplastics behave like any other particulate. Larger fibers (above ~10 micrometers) settle in the upper airways and are typically cleared by mucus and swallowed — turning some inhalation exposure into ingestion exposure. Smaller fragments and nanoplastics can reach the deep lung, where clearance is slow. Particles have been detected in human lung tissue in both upper and lower lobes.

Who is most exposed

  • People who spend long hours in carpeted, poorly ventilated rooms.
  • Textile and recycling workers handling shredded plastic.
  • Commuters in heavy traffic, where tire and brake dust spike.
  • Anyone who line-dries laundry indoors near synthetic fabrics.

Simple ventilation, vacuuming with a HEPA-rated filter, and switching some synthetic textiles for cotton or wool reduce indoor exposure noticeably.

Route 3: Dermal contact — the smallest door

Intact human skin is a strong barrier. Microplastics larger than about 100 nanometers do not pass through it under normal conditions. That makes dermal contact the smallest of the three pathways for most people. Skin still matters in two narrow cases:

  1. Damaged skin. Cuts, burns or eczema reduce barrier function, and nanoparticles can enter through wounds.
  2. Cosmetics and microbeads. Products with intentionally added plastic particles raise contact dose. The EU restricted these in 2023; US regulation lags. Even here, most particles wash off rather than absorb.

For practical purposes, dermal exposure is a rounding error compared with what you drink and breathe.

What happens after particles enter

Once inside, microplastics do not all end up in the same place. Particle size is the deciding factor — the smaller the fragment, the further it travels. The difference between micro- and nanoplastics is so important that we covered it in its own piece: Nanoplastics vs Microplastics: Size, Behavior & Why It Matters.

Particle size Where it tends to go
> 150 µm Stays in gut, excreted in stool
10-150 µm Mostly excreted; some uptake by gut immune cells
1-10 µm Can cross gut lining into lymph and blood
< 1 µm (nanoplastics) Reach blood, liver, kidneys, placenta, possibly brain

Particles have now been detected in human blood, placenta, testes, breast milk and — in a 2024 NYU study — in prostate cancer tumors. We cover the blood findings in Microplastics in Human Blood: What the 2024-2025 Studies Found and the tumor study in Microplastics Found in Prostate Cancer Tumors: What the New NYU Study Means.

How daily intake adds up

A typical American adult takes in roughly 70,000-120,000 microplastic particles per year through food and drink, plus another 50,000-90,000 through breathing, according to a 2019 University of Victoria meta-analysis. People who drink primarily bottled water can add several million additional particles annually on top of that baseline.

The takeaway is not panic — it is leverage. If 60-80% of daily exposure comes through drinking, that is the lever with the highest return. Reducing inhalation requires changing your home and city. Reducing ingestion can start with a single bottle of water.

How to reduce your biggest exposure route

Because drinking dominates the ingestion category, and ingestion dominates total exposure, this is where small changes pay off most. A few practical steps:

  • Filter what you already drink. A cap-style microplastic filter that screws onto a standard bottle removes particles down to 0.2 microns at the moment of drinking.
  • Avoid hot liquids in plastic. Heat accelerates shedding — details in Hot Car Water Bottles: Does Heat Release More Plastic?.
  • Skip plastic-bag teabags or switch to loose leaf.
  • Ventilate and vacuum to cut inhaled fibers.
  • Choose glass or stainless for home storage; see PET vs HDPE vs Glass: Which Bottle Type Is Safest?.

Bottom line

Microplastics enter the human body through three doors, but they are not the same size. Ingestion — especially bottled water — is the front door and accounts for most of your daily intake. Inhalation is the side door, smaller but persistent in indoor environments. Dermal contact is barely a crack. Focus your attention where the load is, and the math takes care of itself.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the main way microplastics enter the human body?

Ingestion is the dominant route, accounting for roughly 60 to 80 percent of daily exposure. Bottled water is the single largest contributor for most adults, followed by tap water, sea salt, seafood and packaged food.

Can microplastics enter the body through the skin?

Intact skin blocks almost all microplastics. Only damaged skin or very small nanoparticles below 100 nanometers may cross the barrier, and the contribution to total exposure stays below two percent.

How many microplastic particles do we breathe in each day?

Estimates put inhalation at roughly 130 to 250 particles per day for a typical adult, mostly indoors. Synthetic textile fibers, carpet dust and tire wear are the main sources.

Do swallowed microplastics stay in the body?

Most particles larger than about 150 micrometers pass through the gut and are excreted. Smaller fragments, especially nanoplastics under one micrometer, can cross the intestinal wall and reach blood, organs and tissues.

What is the easiest way to reduce microplastic intake?

Filter what you drink. Because bottled and tap water drive most ingestion exposure, a fine-pore filter at the point of drinking cuts the largest single source faster than changes to diet, home or commute.

Are children more exposed than adults?

Per kilogram of body weight, yes. Infants in particular show higher counts because of bottle feeding with plastic bottles and crawling on textile floors. They also breathe faster, increasing inhaled dose relative to body size.
