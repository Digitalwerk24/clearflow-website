Microplastics in Human Blood: What Recent Studies Actually Detected

Plastic particles have been measured in human blood, placentas, testicles, brains, arteries, and breast milk between 2022 and 2025 — here's what the data shows

February 15, 2025 9 min read Sources: Environment Int., NEJM, Tox. Sciences

TL;DR: Plastic particles have been measured in human blood, placentas, testicles, brains, arteries, and breast milk between 2022 and 2025. The findings are real, but the health effects are still being studied. Here is what the data shows — and what it does not.

Kurz: Since the first Dutch blood study in 2022, peer-reviewed research has detected microplastics and nanoplastics in human blood, placentas, testicles, brain tissue, atherosclerotic plaques, and breast milk. The detection is now considered settled science. What is still being investigated is how much harm these particles cause, at what dose, and for whom. This page reviews the studies, the methods, and the open questions.

Why the blood findings changed the conversation

For years, microplastics were treated as an environmental issue. They were in oceans, in fish, in soil. Then in March 2022, researchers at Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam published the first study to detect plastic particles in human blood. That single paper shifted the topic from "environmental pollution" to "internal exposure." If plastics travel in the bloodstream, they can in principle reach any organ the blood reaches.

The 2022 Amsterdam blood study: the starting point

The original paper, published in Environment International by Heather Leslie and colleagues, analyzed blood samples from 22 anonymous healthy donors. Plastic particles were quantifiable in 17 of them — roughly 77 percent. The mean concentration was 1.6 micrograms per milliliter of blood.

Four polymer types were identified:

  • PET (polyethylene terephthalate) — the polymer used in disposable water bottles
  • Polystyrene — common in food packaging and disposable cups
  • Polyethylene — plastic bags, films, and bottle caps
  • Poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) — acrylic plastics, dental materials

The detection limit was around 700 nanometers, meaning anything smaller than that was invisible to the method. The real total was almost certainly higher than what was measured. You can read the open-access paper on the publisher's site, and a plain-language summary is also available via Wikipedia's microplastics entry.

What 2024 and 2025 studies added

Since 2022, multiple research groups have replicated and extended the blood finding. The picture in 2025 is no longer about "are particles there" but "how much, what kind, and where do they go."

Brain tissue (University of New Mexico, 2024-2025)

Matthew Campen's team at the University of New Mexico analyzed brain tissue from autopsy samples. Brains collected in 2024 contained roughly 50 percent more microplastic mass than brains collected in 2016 — about 4,800 micrograms per gram of tissue on average, or roughly 0.5 percent of brain weight by mass. The dominant polymer was polyethylene. Samples from people with documented dementia showed three to five times higher concentrations than samples from people without dementia, though causality has not been established.

Placentas (multiple groups, 2021-2024)

Microplastics in human placentas were first reported by Antonio Ragusa's group in Italy in 2021. By 2024, follow-up work confirmed the finding across continents and quantified it: every placenta tested in a 62-sample US series contained measurable microplastic, ranging from about 6.5 to 790 micrograms per gram of tissue. Polyethylene and PVC dominated.

Testicles (2024)

A University of New Mexico study published in Toxicological Sciences in May 2024 examined testicular tissue from 23 human autopsies and 47 pet dogs. Every human sample contained microplastics — a mean of 329 micrograms per gram, nearly three times the canine concentration. Twelve polymer types were identified.

Arteries and cardiovascular events (NEJM, 2024)

A March 2024 study in the New England Journal of Medicine followed 257 patients who underwent surgery to remove plaque from carotid arteries. Microplastics and nanoplastics were found in the plaque of about 58 percent of them. Over a 34-month follow-up, those patients had a 4.5-times higher risk of heart attack, stroke, or death than patients whose plaque tested clean. This is the strongest clinical-outcome signal published so far.

Breast milk, semen, and other tissues

Smaller studies between 2022 and 2024 have detected plastic particles in breast milk, semen, saliva, lung tissue, liver, kidneys, and stool. Detection is now routine across compartments — the methods just need to be sensitive enough.

How researchers actually find particles in blood

Detection is harder than it sounds. Plastic is everywhere in a lab — gloves, tubing, even the air. Modern microplastic studies use strict protocols to avoid false positives.

  1. Sample collection in glass containers, never plastic, with metal needles and PTFE-coated tubing
  2. Chemical digestion of the biological tissue using potassium hydroxide or enzymes, which dissolves cells but leaves polymers intact
  3. Filtration through fine metal or anodisc filters, often down to 200 nanometers
  4. Chemical identification using pyrolysis-GC/MS, Raman spectroscopy, or FTIR — methods that fingerprint the polymer, not just count particles
  5. Blank controls processed in parallel to subtract background contamination

The standard limitation in nearly every study is the lower size cutoff. Most methods cannot reliably detect particles below 100-700 nanometers — yet those are the particles most likely to cross biological barriers. So almost every published number is an undercount of the true plastic load.

What the studies did NOT find

It is important to be precise about what is and is not known. The papers above prove presence. They do not, by themselves, prove harm in healthy adults. Specifically:

  • No study has identified a safe blood concentration or a toxic threshold
  • No randomized human trial has linked microplastic dose to a specific disease
  • The NEJM artery study shows correlation with cardiovascular events, not proven causation
  • The brain study shows accumulation over time, not proven cognitive damage
  • No regulatory agency, including the FDA or EPA, has yet set a microplastic exposure limit for drinking water

How particles likely reach the bloodstream

Three exposure routes account for most of the internal load:

  • Ingestion — bottled water, seafood, salt, tea bags, food packaging. The dominant source for most adults.
  • Inhalation — indoor air, synthetic textiles, road dust. Particles below 2.5 micrometers can reach deep lung tissue.
  • Dermal contact — minor compared to the other two; intact skin blocks most particles.

Bottled water alone delivers around 240,000 plastic particles per liter on average, according to a 2024 Columbia University study using stimulated Raman scattering microscopy. Roughly 90 percent of those were in the nanoplastic range — small enough in principle to cross the gut wall. What this means for ordinary consumers

The honest answer in 2025: every adult tested so far carries some plastic load. The total mass is small — micrograms to milligrams in most tissues — but the trend across decades is upward, the polymer types match consumer plastics, and the first clinical-outcome signals are now appearing.

The reasonable response is not panic. It is reducing the exposure routes you can actually control. Bottled water is the biggest single source for most Americans. Heated food in plastic containers is the second. Synthetic textile dust is third. None of these require lifestyle overhauls — they require swaps.

Open research questions for 2025-2026

Several large studies are now in progress and worth watching:

  1. Whether nanoplastic concentration in blood correlates with specific inflammatory markers
  2. Whether placental microplastics correlate with birth weight or pregnancy complications
  3. Whether the brain accumulation seen in 2024 is reversible if exposure drops
  4. What blood concentration, if any, qualifies as "high exposure" worth clinical attention
  5. Whether ultra-processed-food consumption predicts blood plastic load

None of these questions have clean answers yet. They will probably be answered between 2026 and 2030.

The bottom line

Microplastics in human blood are not theoretical. They are detectable, repeatable across labs, and accumulating across tissues. The studies between 2022 and 2025 closed the question of presence. The question of harm is still open, but the early cardiovascular and neurological signals are serious enough that exposure reduction makes sense even before the final answers arrive. Reduce what you can control, and let the research do the rest.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are microplastics really in human blood?

Yes. The first detection was published by Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam in 2022, finding plastic particles in 77 percent of 22 healthy donors. Multiple labs have since replicated the result across continents.

How do microplastics get into the bloodstream?

Mostly through ingestion of food and bottled water, secondarily through inhalation of indoor air and synthetic textile dust. Particles small enough — generally under one micrometer — can cross the gut lining or lung tissue and reach blood vessels.

Can microplastics in blood cause disease?

Causation is not proven, but a 2024 New England Journal of Medicine study found patients with microplastic-containing arterial plaque had 4.5 times the risk of heart attack, stroke, or death over 34 months. The correlation is real; clinical trials to confirm causation are still needed.

What polymers are most often found in blood?

PET from drink bottles, polystyrene from food packaging, polyethylene from films and caps, and PMMA from acrylics and dental materials are the four most commonly detected. PET is the most consistent finding across studies.

Can the body remove microplastics from blood?

Some particles are excreted in stool and urine, but a portion appears to deposit in tissues like brain, liver, and placenta over time. No medical treatment currently exists to actively clear microplastics from the bloodstream.

Does filtering bottled water reduce blood microplastic load?

No human trial has measured this directly yet. Reducing intake from bottled water — the largest single ingestion source for many adults — is expected to lower ongoing exposure, but existing blood concentration depends on cumulative lifetime intake.
