How Much Microplastic Do You Eat Per Week? The Credit-Card Study, Explained

The viral "credit card per week" stat comes from one 2019 study — here's what it actually measured and what's realistic

January 15, 2026 8 min read Sources: WWF, Newcastle, Columbia

TL;DR: You've probably heard you eat a credit card's worth of plastic every week — about 5 grams. That figure comes from a single 2019 WWF-commissioned analysis by the University of Newcastle. The real answer is messier, the biggest source is drinking water, and newer estimates run from a few milligrams to several grams per week.

Kurz: The viral "credit card a week" claim traces back to one 2019 review by the University of Newcastle, commissioned by WWF. It modeled — not directly measured — weekly plastic intake at roughly 5 grams. Newer studies put the range anywhere from a few milligrams to several grams. The biggest single contributor in almost every estimate is the water we drink.

The credit-card statistic is everywhere: in headlines, on packaging, in TED talks. It's a sticky number because it's vivid. But the science behind it is younger and shakier than the meme suggests. This page is part of our broader hub on Microplastics Science: Sources, Exposure & Latest Research, where we trace every major claim about plastic exposure back to the study it came from.

Where the "5 grams a week" number actually comes from

The figure traces back to a 2019 report titled No Plastic in Nature: Assessing Plastic Ingestion from Nature to People, commissioned by the World Wide Fund for Nature (WWF) and prepared by researchers at the University of Newcastle in Australia. The team didn't run a single new lab experiment. They did something subtler — and more controversial. They pulled together more than 50 existing studies on microplastics in food, drink, and air, then modeled how much an average person might ingest.

The result: about 2,000 microplastic particles per week, weighing roughly 5 grams. A standard credit card weighs about 5 grams. The communications team had its headline.

The report is publicly available on the WWF website, and the methodology paper from Newcastle was later published in peer-reviewed form. It's a real study — but it's a modeling exercise, not a direct measurement of anyone's stomach contents.

What the study actually measured (and what it didn't)

The Newcastle team focused on a narrow slice of sources where reliable data existed. Each category was assigned a per-capita intake range, then summed.

Source Estimated share of weekly intake Data confidence
Drinking water (bottled + tap) ~1,769 particles Strong — multiple studies
Shellfish ~182 particles Moderate
Salt ~11 particles Moderate
Beer ~10 particles Limited
Air (inhaled, deposited in food) Variable, often excluded Weak at the time

Notice what's missing: produce, dairy, meat, tea, rice, sugar, processed foods, and indoor air dust. The study only counted sources with usable published data in 2018. That's a feature of careful research — but it also means the 5-gram figure is built on a partial map. Newer studies on Microplastics in Food: Salt, Seafood, Tea Bags & Produce suggest tea bags alone may release billions of nanoparticles per cup, which wasn't in the original model.

Why drinking water dominated the estimate

Roughly 88% of the modeled weekly particles came from one source: water. That's both bottled and tap, with bottled water carrying a much higher per-liter load. A 2018 Orb Media analysis of bottled water brands found an average of 325 microplastic particles per liter — and a 2024 Columbia University nanoplastics study put the figure for bottled water into the hundreds of thousands of particles per liter once you count particles smaller than one micron.

If you want to see how those numbers shift the per-week math, our page on Microplastics in Bottled Water: What the Studies Actually Show walks through the major brand studies side by side. The short version: the more sensitive the instrument, the more plastic researchers find. Older studies counted only what they could see under a microscope. Newer methods catch nanoplastics — and the totals explode.

The pushback: why some researchers say 5 grams is too high

Not everyone bought the credit-card claim. In 2021, a team at the University of Victoria published a recalculated estimate that put weekly intake closer to 0.1 to 5 milligrams — roughly a thousand times lower. Their argument: the original model converted particle counts into mass using assumptions about average particle size and density that may have overshot.

The discrepancy comes down to three things:

  1. Particle size assumptions. A microplastic isn't one size. The Newcastle model used median estimates; critics argued most ingested particles are far smaller and lighter.
  2. Density assumptions. Different polymers (PET, polyethylene, polystyrene) have different weights. Aggregating them is rough.
  3. Counting vs weighing. Modern detection counts particles. Translating counts into grams introduces large error bars.

So which number is right? Honestly, neither — yet. The 5-gram figure is probably an over-estimate of mass. The microgram estimates are probably an under-estimate of particle count. Both can be true.

How the picture has changed since 2019

Several developments have reshaped the conversation:

  • Nanoplastics entered the chat. The 2024 Columbia/Rutgers nanoplastics paper used a new Raman scattering technique and found 240,000 plastic fragments per liter of bottled water — 90% of them nanoplastics smaller than one micron. The original Newcastle model couldn't see most of these. We unpack the size question in detail on Nanoplastics vs Microplastics: Size, Behavior & Why It Matters.
  • Blood and tissue studies. Microplastics have now been documented in human blood, placentas, testicles, and tumors. See our breakdown of Microplastics in Human Blood: What the 2024-2025 Studies Found for the actual lab findings.
  • Better exposure mapping. Researchers now track multiple pathways — see How Microplastics Enter the Human Body: Pathways Explained for ingestion, inhalation, and dermal routes.

None of this proves the credit-card figure exactly. It does suggest that whatever the true mass, the particle count reaching the human body each week is probably much higher than 2,000.

What a realistic weekly estimate looks like in 2026

If you average across the credible studies published between 2019 and 2025, a defensible range looks like this:

Metric Conservative Central Higher-end
Particles per week ~2,000 ~50,000 >1,000,000 (incl. nanoplastics)
Mass per week 0.1 mg 10–50 mg ~5 g
Biggest source Water Water + food Water + air + food

The honest takeaway: nobody knows the exact mass. But every recent study agrees on the direction — exposure is real, ongoing, and largely driven by what we drink. What you can actually do about it

If water drives most exposure, water is where small changes pay off most. A few options:

  1. Switch some bottled water to filtered tap. Tap water generally has far fewer particles per liter than bottled, especially for nanoplastics.

The bottom line

The credit-card-a-week stat is half-true and fully useful. The exact mass is debated. The exposure is not. The original 2019 Newcastle study did the field a favor by giving the public a tangible image — and a starting point that researchers have been refining ever since. Whether your real weekly intake is 5 milligrams or 5 grams, the practical levers are the same: cut the highest-shedding sources, and filter the water you can't avoid drinking from plastic.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do we really eat a credit card's worth of plastic every week?

That figure comes from one 2019 WWF-commissioned modeling study by the University of Newcastle. It's a real estimate, but it's a modeled number, not a direct measurement. Other researchers have recalculated it as much lower in mass while counting many more particles. The true weekly intake is somewhere between a few milligrams and several grams.

What is the biggest source of microplastics we ingest?

Drinking water — both bottled and tap — accounts for the largest share in every major estimate, roughly 88 percent of weekly particles in the Newcastle model. Bottled water carries a far higher per-liter load than tap, especially once nanoplastics are counted.

Who funded the credit-card study?

The 2019 report was commissioned by WWF (World Wide Fund for Nature) and produced by researchers at the University of Newcastle in Australia. The underlying methodology was later published in peer-reviewed form.

Has the 5 grams per week figure been confirmed by other studies?

Not directly. A 2021 University of Victoria reanalysis estimated weekly intake closer to 0.1 to 5 milligrams in mass — about a thousand times less. But newer detection methods that can see nanoplastics suggest the particle count is much higher than the original 2,000 per week.

Can I reduce how much microplastic I consume?

Yes, mostly by changing what you drink and how it's stored. Filtering bottled or tap water removes a large share of microplastic particles. Switching from bottled to filtered tap water for daily hydration is the single highest-impact change, since water is the dominant exposure source.

Does the credit-card stat include nanoplastics?

No. The 2019 study only counted particles its source studies could detect, which were mostly in the micrometer range. Newer nanoplastics research has found hundreds of thousands of sub-micron particles per liter of bottled water, none of which were captured in the original 5-gram model.
