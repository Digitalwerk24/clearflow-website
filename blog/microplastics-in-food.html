Microplastics in Food: What You're Actually Eating

Salt, seafood, tea bags, produce and packaging — what the data shows about microplastics on your plate

January 15, 2025 8 min read Sources: Environ. Sci. Technol., Nature Sustainability, WHO

TL;DR: Microplastics show up in salt, seafood, tea, produce, honey and packaged foods — not just bottled water. Sea salt and tea bags are surprisingly high, while shellfish and rice top the seafood and grain lists. Reducing exposure is mostly about packaging choices, not panic.

Kurz: Microplastics are not only a water problem. They turn up in table salt, mussels, plastic tea bags, fruit, vegetables and almost every food that touches plastic packaging. This guide walks through the foods with the highest measured loads, where the particles come from, and which swaps actually move the needle. For the wider science picture, see our hub on Microplastics Science: Sources, Exposure & Latest Research.

Most people first hear about microplastics through bottled water headlines. But peer-reviewed surveys make one thing clear: your plate matters as much as your glass. A 2019 review by the World Wildlife Fund estimated humans ingest up to a credit card's worth of plastic per week — and food, not just water, drives that number. We unpack the math in How Much Microplastic Do You Eat Per Week? Credit-Card Study Explained, but here we focus on the foods themselves.

Why food is a bigger exposure route than people think

Microplastics enter food in three main ways. First, environmental contamination: plastic debris in oceans, rivers and soil breaks down into fragments that organisms absorb. Second, processing: industrial equipment, conveyor belts and plastic-lined tanks shed particles. Third, packaging: plastic films, bottles, pouches and tea bags release microplastics directly into the product they contain, especially under heat. The full pathway map lives in How Microplastics Enter the Human Body: Pathways Explained.

The size distribution matters too. Most food studies count particles larger than 10 micrometers, but the smaller fraction — nanoplastics — is where the toxicological concern is highest. We compare them in detail in Nanoplastics vs Microplastics: Size, Behavior & Why It Matters.

Salt: the surprise winner

Salt is one of the most heavily contaminated foods on a per-gram basis. A 2018 study in Environmental Science & Technology tested 39 salt brands from 21 countries and found microplastics in 90 percent of them. Sea salt was the worst, with averages around 600 particles per kilogram, followed by lake salt and rock salt.

Salt type Median microplastics (particles/kg) Main polymer found
Sea salt ~600 Polyethylene, PET
Lake salt ~200 Polypropylene
Rock salt ~40 Polyethylene
Refined table salt ~30 Mixed

The reason is straightforward: sea salt is evaporated from ocean water, and oceans now carry an estimated 170 trillion plastic particles. The salt simply concentrates what's already floating. Rock salt mined from ancient deposits is the lowest, since it predates the plastic era.

Seafood: shellfish lead the list

Filter feeders are the highest-exposure category. Mussels, oysters and clams pump huge volumes of water across their gills, and they retain microplastic fragments in their tissue. Studies from the North Sea and the US Pacific coast typically report 0.2 to 4 particles per gram of mussel flesh — and because you eat the whole animal, gut included, the dose is delivered directly.

Fish are different. Most microplastics accumulate in the gut, which you discard. A fillet of cod, tuna or salmon usually carries far less than a mussel of equivalent weight. The exception is small whole-eaten fish like sardines and anchovies, where the digestive tract goes with the meal.

  • Highest: mussels, oysters, clams, sardines
  • Medium: shrimp (especially with shells), squid
  • Lowest: fillets of large fish where the gut is removed

Tea bags: the plastic you boil

This one shocks people. A 2019 McGill University study found that a single plastic tea bag steeped at brewing temperature released roughly 11.6 billion microplastic and 3.1 billion nanoplastic particles into one cup. Many premium "silky" pyramid bags are made from nylon or PET — not paper. Heat plus agitation is the worst-case scenario for particle shedding.

One plastic tea bag at brewing temperature can release more particles in a single cup than most people drink from bottled water in a week.

The fix is easy: switch to loose-leaf tea with a stainless-steel infuser, or buy brands that explicitly use unbleached paper or PLA-free bags. Check the box; manufacturers rarely advertise that the mesh is plastic.

Produce: roots and leaves absorb particles

Plants don't just get coated with microplastics from the air — they take them up through the roots. A 2020 study in Nature Sustainability showed lettuce and wheat absorb nanoplastics from contaminated soil and translocate them into stems and leaves. Carrots, radishes and other root vegetables are particularly affected because the edible part sits in soil that often contains plastic mulch fragments, tire dust and sewage-sludge fertilizer.

Apples and pears, surprisingly, ranked high in one Italian survey, likely because of long transport in plastic crates and atmospheric deposition. Washing produce removes surface particles but not what the plant has already absorbed.

Food packaging: the underrated source

Packaging may be the single largest food-borne route. Every time you peel a plastic film, microwave a polypropylene container, or open a sealed pouch, you generate fragments. A 2023 study found that a single act of opening a plastic bottle cap releases hundreds of particles into the surrounding food or liquid.

High-risk packaging behaviors include:

  1. Microwaving food in plastic containers — heat dramatically increases migration
  2. Storing acidic or oily foods in plastic for long periods
  3. Cutting on plastic cutting boards, which a 2023 study estimated can shed 14 to 71 million microplastics per person per year
  4. Drinking hot coffee from disposable cups, which are lined with polyethylene
  5. Reusing single-use bottles repeatedly without cleaning — more on this in Refilling Plastic Bottles Safely: How Many Times Is Okay?

Processed and packaged foods

Highly processed foods compound the problem because each processing step touches plastic equipment. Studies have measured microplastics in honey, sugar, beer, milk, infant formula and rice. Plastic-packaged rice can carry up to four times more particles than rice sold loose. A 2024 study in Journal of Hazardous Materials reported that polypropylene infant feeding bottles released 1.6 million particles per liter when used to prepare formula at recommended temperatures.

How food compares to water

Honest framing matters. Bottled water is one source among many. Recent estimates suggest food contributes a larger share of total ingestion than water for most adults, but bottled water is the easiest single source to address because the fix is mechanical: filter it. Practical swaps that actually work

You won't eliminate microplastics. You can cut them substantially with a handful of habit changes:

  • Buy loose-leaf tea or paper-bag brands; avoid nylon pyramids
  • Use a wooden or end-grain cutting board instead of plastic
  • Reheat food in glass or ceramic, never plastic
  • Choose rock salt or refined table salt for cooking; save sea salt for finishing
  • Eat fish fillets more often than whole shellfish if you eat seafood weekly
  • Wash produce; peel root vegetables grown on industrial farms
  • Filter bottled or tap water before drinking — see our full portable filter guide

The bottom line

Food is a bigger microplastics route than most headlines suggest. Salt, shellfish, plastic-bagged tea and processed foods carry the heaviest measured loads, while packaging behaviors quietly drive much of the rest. The good news: a few specific swaps — loose tea, glass storage, wood cutting boards, filtered water — cut exposure meaningfully without requiring you to overhaul your diet. Frequently Asked Questions

Which food has the most microplastics?

On a per-gram basis, sea salt and plastic-bag tea rank highest in measured studies. Mussels and oysters lead the seafood category because you eat the whole animal, gut included. Highly processed and plastic-packaged foods often deliver the largest cumulative weekly dose.

Do tea bags really release plastic?

Yes. Pyramid-style silky tea bags made from nylon or PET can release billions of microplastic and nanoplastic particles per cup when steeped at brewing temperature. Switching to loose-leaf tea or unbleached paper bags eliminates this source.

Does cooking remove microplastics from food?

No. Heat does not destroy microplastics. In fact, heating food in plastic containers usually increases particle migration into the food. Boiling water can reduce some particles in hard-water situations but does not solve food contamination.

Should I stop eating seafood because of microplastics?

Not necessarily. Fish fillets carry relatively low loads because the gut, where most particles accumulate, is removed. Filter feeders like mussels, oysters and clams are higher-exposure since you eat the whole animal.

Is plastic-packaged food safe to eat?

Plastic packaging is approved for food contact but does shed particles, especially under heat, acidity or long storage. The simplest reduction is to avoid microwaving in plastic and to transfer leftovers to glass or ceramic containers.

Can washing produce remove microplastics?

Washing removes surface-deposited particles but not microplastics that the plant has absorbed through its roots. Peeling root vegetables grown on industrial farms helps further, since plastic mulch and sewage-sludge fertilizers can contaminate the soil layer.
