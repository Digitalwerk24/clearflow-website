Distillation vs Filtration: Which Actually Removes Microplastics?

Both remove microplastics — but they solve different problems. Here's the practical breakdown.

February 10, 2025 8 min read Sources: EPA, WHO

TL;DR: Both distillation and filtration remove microplastics, but they solve different problems. Distillation is the most thorough lab-grade method. Physical filtration is faster, cheaper, and the only realistic option for the bottle you carry around all day.

Kurz: Distillation boils water and captures only the pure steam, leaving microplastics behind. Filtration pushes water through a membrane fine enough to trap plastic particles. Distillation is more thorough on paper, but it's slow, energy-hungry, and impossible on the go. For the plastic bottle in your hand right now, filtration wins on practicality.

If you've been reading about microplastics in bottled water, you've probably hit the same fork in the road: should you distill your water or filter it? Both approaches show up in research papers, both have loyal fans, and both are marketed as the "only real solution." The honest answer is that they aren't really competing for the same job. This guide breaks down how each method actually works, what it can and cannot do, what it costs, and when one beats the other. For the underlying science of pore sizes and membranes, see our parent guide on Microplastic Filtration Technology: How Filters Actually Work.

What Distillation Actually Does

Distillation is one of the oldest water purification methods on earth. You heat water until it turns to steam, then collect that steam in a separate chamber where it cools back into liquid. Anything heavier than a water molecule — minerals, salts, metals, bacteria, and yes, microplastics — gets left behind in the boiling chamber.

The science is straightforward. Water boils at 100°C (212°F). Polyethylene terephthalate (PET), the plastic most bottled water comes in, melts around 250°C and doesn't vaporize at boiling temperatures. Polyethylene starts to degrade around 105°C but its particles are far too heavy to travel with water vapor. So when steam rises, microplastic particles physically cannot follow. They stay in the pot.

That's why distilled water shows up in research labs, hospitals, and industrial applications where contamination tolerance is essentially zero. The U.S. EPA notes that distillation removes a broader spectrum of contaminants than almost any other single-step method, including dissolved solids that most filters cannot touch. See the EPA's home water treatment overview for the broader context.

The Catch With Distillation

Distillation works beautifully — in a kitchen, with hours to spare and a power outlet. A typical countertop distiller produces about 1 gallon every 4-6 hours and draws 750-800 watts the entire time. That's roughly 3-4 kWh per gallon, or 30-50 cents of electricity depending on your rate. The unit itself runs $150-$400.

You also have to clean the residue. Every gallon of distilled water leaves behind a layer of brown-yellow scale where minerals, organics, and microplastic particles concentrate. Skip the cleaning and the next batch tastes off. And distilled water itself tastes flat — many people add minerals back in or find it unpleasant to drink straight.

What Filtration Actually Does

Filtration is mechanical. Water is pushed through a barrier with holes small enough to block contaminants but large enough to let H₂O molecules through. For microplastics specifically, the relevant filters are membranes with pore sizes between 0.01 and 0.2 microns. Anything bigger than that pore — and microplastics are by definition between 1 nanometer and 5 millimeters — gets stuck on the membrane while clean water passes.

Most credible portable microplastic filters use either hollow fiber ultrafiltration membranes or pleated polymer membranes. The pore size determines what gets caught. A 0.2-micron filter blocks all microplastics, all bacteria, and most protozoa. A 0.01-micron ultrafilter goes further and catches viruses and the smallest nanoplastic particles. For the full breakdown, our Filter Pore Size Chart: Microns, Nanometers & What They Block shows what each pore size blocks.

What Filtration Doesn't Do

Here's where honesty matters. A physical filter sized for microplastics will not remove dissolved heavy metals like lead, chlorine, fluoride, pharmaceuticals, or PFAS "forever chemicals." Those are dissolved at the molecular level and slip right through any membrane built for particles. If you need those out, you need activated carbon, ion exchange, reverse osmosis, or — yes — distillation.

This is the single biggest source of marketing confusion in the water filter category. A filter that's excellent at one job is often advertised as if it does every job. When Distillation Makes Sense

Distillation isn't a bad choice. It's just a specific one. It earns its place when:

  • You're treating water of unknown quality at home — well water with metals, or a region with industrial contamination.
  • You need water for medical devices like CPAP machines, humidifiers, or sterilization, where any residue causes problems.
  • You want one device that handles essentially every type of contaminant in a single step.
  • You don't mind the wait time and the kitchen counter footprint.

For people on private wells or in homes with old lead service lines, distillation or reverse osmosis are genuinely the right answer. A microplastic cap filter is not.

When Filtration Makes Sense

Filtration wins everywhere distillation can't follow you. That's most of life:

  1. The bottle in your gym bag, your car, your office desk.
  2. The bottled water you buy at the airport, a stadium, a convenience store.
  3. Travel — hotels, conferences, road trips.
  4. Any moment where you're drinking from a sealed plastic bottle and want to remove the microplastics it sheds without changing your habits.

This is the entire reason cap-style filters exist. The water in most U.S. bottled brands is already treated for chemical contaminants — what it picks up between the bottling plant and your mouth is microplastic particles from the bottle itself, the cap, and storage temperature swings. A 0.2-micron membrane handles exactly that, in seconds, with no power, no waiting, and no kitchen real estate.

The "Why Not Just Distill Everything?" Question

It comes up in every comparison thread: if distillation is more thorough, why isn't it the default? Three reasons.

First, throughput. The average American drinks 3-4 liters of water a day. Distilling that volume daily means running an appliance for 8+ hours and remembering to refill, clean, and store the output. Most households drop the habit within weeks.

Second, the problem isn't usually that severe. For municipal tap water that already meets EPA standards, the main concern is microplastics and chlorine taste. Both are solvable with a far simpler setup. For deeper context on what's actually in U.S. drinking water, see Microplastics in Tap Water: How They Get In and What You Can Do and our full Microplastics in Drinking Water — The Complete Guide guide.

Third, mineral loss. Calcium and magnesium in drinking water contribute meaningfully to daily intake. Distilled water strips them all out. The World Health Organization has flagged long-term consumption of fully demineralized water as a low but real concern, recommending remineralization for drinking-water applications.

What If You Want Both?

Plenty of households do. They run a distiller or reverse osmosis system at home for cooking and drinking, and they carry a cap filter on the bottled water they buy outside. That stack covers nearly every scenario without any single device pretending to be all things. The Practical Pick

If you only drink from a tap you trust and you want zero contaminants, get a distiller. If you drink bottled water at all — which most Americans do, multiple times a week — distillation isn't going to help you in the moment that matters. The bottle is already sealed, the microplastics are already shedding, and you're not bringing a distiller to a softball game.

That's the moment a cap filter solves. The same standard plastic bottle, twisted onto a 0.2-micron ultrafilter, and the microplastic problem is handled before the first sip. Filtration is the everyday tool for the bottles you actually drink from. Pick the method that matches where you'll be when you're thirsty.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does distilled water have microplastics?

Properly distilled water has essentially no microplastics, because plastic particles cannot evaporate with steam. The catch is storage: if distilled water is stored in a plastic container afterward, it can pick up new microplastic particles from the bottle itself.

Is distillation better than filtration for microplastics?

On removal percentage, yes — distillation removes nearly 100% of microplastics plus most other contaminants. On practicality, filtration wins, because it takes seconds, requires no electricity, and works on bottled water anywhere.

Can you distill plastic particles out of bottled water?

Yes. If you pour bottled water into a distiller, the microplastic particles stay in the boiling chamber while pure steam condenses into clean water. But this defeats the convenience of bottled water entirely and only makes sense at home.

What does distillation not remove?

Distillation can fail to remove volatile organic compounds (VOCs) that vaporize at similar temperatures to water, such as some chlorine byproducts and certain pesticides. That's why many distillers pair the steam path with an activated carbon stage.

Is filtered water as safe as distilled water?

For microplastics specifically, a 0.2-micron or finer filter is comparable to distilled water. For heavy metals and dissolved chemicals, distilled water removes more unless your filter explicitly includes activated carbon or reverse osmosis stages.

Does boiling water work like distillation?

No. Boiling kills bacteria but leaves microplastics in the water — they don't evaporate and they don't break down at boiling temperature. Some studies even suggest boiling can release additional particles from the pot.
