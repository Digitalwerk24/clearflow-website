How Long Bottled Water Actually Lasts (And When to Toss It)

Sealed bottled water doesn't truly expire — but the plastic around it does. Here's what the date really means.

January 15, 2025 8 min read Sources: FDA, PNAS

TL;DR: Sealed bottled water doesn't truly expire, but the plastic does. Most brands print a 1–2 year date because flavor changes and bottles can leach more microplastics over time, especially with heat. Storage is what really matters.

Kurz: Sealed bottled water is shelf-stable for years on paper, but the bottle around it isn't. Manufacturers print a date — usually 1 to 2 years out — because plastic affects taste, lets in trace odors, and slowly sheds more particles the longer and hotter it sits. The water itself rarely "goes bad." The container is the clock.

This guide breaks down what the printed date really means, how long different bottled waters actually last in a pantry, garage, or hot car, and when old water is fine versus when it's worth replacing. It's part of our hub on Plastic Bottles, Brands & Smarter Drinking Choices, which covers the full picture of buying, storing, and drinking bottled water with less plastic exposure.

Does Bottled Water Actually Expire?

Pure water — H₂O — doesn't spoil. There's nothing in it for bacteria to feed on the way milk or juice has sugars and proteins. So the short answer is: water itself does not expire in the food-safety sense.

But what you buy at the store isn't just water. It's water inside a plastic bottle, sealed with a plastic cap, with a tiny pocket of air at the top. That whole system has a useful life. Once the seal is broken, or once the bottle has been sitting warm for months, the chemistry inside changes in small but measurable ways.

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration, which regulates bottled water as a packaged food, does not require an expiration date on bottled water at all. According to the FDA's bottled water guidance, the agency considers properly stored, sealed bottled water to have an indefinite safety shelf life. The dates you see on the label are voluntary, set by the bottler.

Why Brands Still Print a Date

If the FDA doesn't demand it, why do almost all bottled water brands stamp on a "best by" date? Three reasons, and none of them are about the water turning toxic.

  1. Inventory and rotation. Retailers and distributors need a date to track stock and pull old pallets. It's a logistics tool first.
  2. Taste degradation. Water can pick up faint plastic, cardboard, or chemical notes from the bottle and its environment. A 1–2 year date sets a reasonable window for the product tasting as intended.
  3. Plastic aging. PET (the clear plastic in most bottles) slowly breaks down with time, light, and heat. Older bottles can shed slightly more particles into the water — including microplastics. Brands cap shelf life partly to limit that drift.

That last point matters more than most shoppers realize. Typical Shelf Life by Bottle Type

Different bottle materials behave differently. Here's a realistic picture of how long each format stays at its best:

Bottle Type Printed "Best By" Practical Safe Window (Cool, Sealed) Main Limit
Single-use PET (still water) 12–24 months 2+ years Taste, plastic shedding
Single-use PET (sparkling) 9–12 months ~12 months CO₂ loss through plastic
HDPE jugs (1 gal) 6–12 months 1 year Cloudy plastic, taste
Glass bottled water 24+ months 3+ years Cap seal, light exposure
Aluminum cans/bottles 12–24 months 2 years Liner integrity

Glass wins on shelf life because it doesn't leach or breathe. PET is fine for years if kept cool and dark, but it's the most sensitive to temperature. For more on how each material compares on safety, see PET vs HDPE vs Glass: Which Bottle Type Is Safest?.

What Actually Changes in Old Bottled Water

If you crack open a bottle that's been in your basement for three years, here's what's different from a fresh one:

1. Taste and smell

This is the most noticeable shift. Old PET bottles can give water a faint plasticky, slightly stale, or "cardboard" flavor. It's not dangerous, but it's not pleasant. Sparkling water also flattens because CO₂ slowly migrates out through the plastic wall.

2. Plastic particle load

Studies on aged PET bottles consistently find that warmer storage and longer storage both increase the number of micro- and nanoplastic particles in the water. The bottle is shedding into its contents — slowly, but measurably. Heat speeds this up dramatically; cold pantry storage barely moves the needle.

3. Antimony and other trace migrants

PET is made using an antimony catalyst, and tiny amounts can migrate into the water over time. Levels stay well below FDA and EPA limits in normal storage, but they rise with time and temperature. Glass and aluminum sidestep this entirely.

4. Microbial growth (only if opened)

Sealed bottled water stays sterile. Once you open it and put your mouth on it, bacteria from your saliva enter the bottle. An opened bottle left at room temperature for a few days can grow noticeable bacterial loads, especially in summer. This isn't an expiration issue — it's a hygiene one.

How Storage Conditions Make or Break Shelf Life

The single biggest factor in whether bottled water lasts six months or six years is where you keep it. The same case of PET water can age completely differently depending on conditions.

Temperature

Heat is the enemy. Storing bottled water at 100°F instead of 70°F can roughly double the rate of antimony migration and meaningfully increase microplastic shedding. A garage, a car trunk, or a sunny pantry shelf is the worst place to keep a long-term stash. A hot car is even worse — we covered that scenario in detail in our piece on Hot Car Water Bottles: Does Heat Release More Plastic?.

Light

UV light degrades PET and can also encourage trace algal growth on the outside of the bottle. Clear-plastic cases stored near a window age faster than the same cases in a closet.

Odors nearby

PET is slightly permeable. Storing bottled water next to gasoline, paint, cleaning supplies, or strong-smelling foods can transfer those odors into the water within weeks. This is the most common cause of "weird-tasting" emergency water people pull out years later.

Physical handling

Bottles that get dropped, squeezed, or stacked under heavy loads can develop micro-cracks in the cap seal area. Those let in air and shorten useful life.

Signs You Should Toss a Bottle

You don't need a lab to tell when bottled water has overstayed its welcome. Trust your senses:

  • Off smell — plasticky, musty, chemical, or anything other than "nothing."
  • Cloudy or hazy water when it used to be clear.
  • Visible particles, slime, or biofilm, especially in opened bottles.
  • Bulging, deformed, or sticky bottle — usually a heat-damage sign.
  • Cap that's hard to twist or feels brittle.
  • Algae green tint in bottles stored in sunlight.

Any one of these is enough to pour it out. Most are about the container, not the water, which is exactly the point.

How to Actually Store Bottled Water

If you keep bottled water for emergencies, hydration at the office, or just bulk-buy from a warehouse club, a few habits stretch quality to the maximum:

  1. Keep it cool. Aim for 50–70°F. A pantry shelf or interior closet beats a garage every time.
  2. Keep it dark. Original cardboard cases shield bottles from UV. Don't unwrap the case until you're using it.
  3. Keep it away from chemicals. Don't store water in the same closet as cleaning products, fuel, or strong solvents.
  4. Rotate stock. First in, first out. Mark cases with a sharpie when you buy them.
  5. Don't refreeze and thaw. Freezing a bottle once is fine; repeated freeze-thaw cycles stress the plastic.
  6. Once opened, finish within a few days, refrigerated if possible.

Is Old Bottled Water Safe to Drink?

For most people, in most situations: yes. A sealed PET bottle stored in a cool, dark place is safe to drink years past its printed date. The water won't make you sick. It may taste flat or faintly plasticky, and it has accumulated slightly more microplastic particles than a fresh bottle — but it's not spoiled in any meaningful sense.

Where the answer changes:

  • Heat-damaged bottles (hot car, hot garage, attic) — replace these. The plastic load is higher, and taste is usually off.
  • Bottles that already had visible damage (cracked caps, dented sides) — don't drink long-stored ones.
  • Opened bottles older than a few days — toss if they were unrefrigerated.
  • Bottles stored near strong odors — usually undrinkable on taste alone.

What About Reusing or Refilling Old Bottles?

Refilling a single-use PET bottle once or twice with cold water is generally fine. Doing it for months, especially with warm water or dishwasher cycles, accelerates plastic degradation in exactly the same way long storage does — except now you're drinking from it daily. Bottom Line

Bottled water doesn't really expire — but the bottle does. A printed "best by" date is mostly about taste and inventory, not safety. If you store water cool, dark, sealed, and away from odors, it stays drinkable for years. If you store it hot, sunny, or near chemicals, replace it well before the date. And remember: even a fresh bottle has microplastics in it. Shelf life is just one piece of the bigger conversation about smarter bottled water habits.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does bottled water actually expire?

Water itself doesn't expire because nothing in it can spoil. The plastic bottle, however, ages — affecting taste and slowly shedding more particles. The FDA does not require expiration dates on bottled water; the dates you see are voluntary.

Is it safe to drink bottled water that's 5 years old?

If the bottle was sealed and stored cool and dark, it's almost certainly safe. It may taste flat or faintly plasticky and will have slightly higher microplastic content than a fresh bottle. If it was stored in heat or sunlight, replace it.

How long can you keep an open bottle of water?

Once your mouth has touched the bottle, bacteria enter and start multiplying. Drink an opened bottle within a day at room temperature, or up to three to four days if refrigerated.

Why does old bottled water taste like plastic?

PET bottles slowly release trace compounds, including antimony and microplastic particles, that can give the water a stale or plasticky taste. Heat dramatically accelerates this. Glass-bottled water doesn't have the same problem.

What's the best way to store bottled water long term?

Keep it sealed in its original case, between 50 and 70°F, away from sunlight, and far from chemicals or strong odors. Rotate stock first-in, first-out. Avoid garages, car trunks, and attic storage.

Can old bottled water make you sick?

Sealed, properly stored bottled water is very unlikely to make you sick even years past its date. The bigger risks are heat-damaged bottles, opened bottles left unrefrigerated, or water that picked up strong odors during storage.
