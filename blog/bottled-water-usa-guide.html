Bottled Water Consumer Guide USA

The American Bottled Water Guide: What's Really in That Bottle

Labels decoded, brands compared, FDA rules explained — and smarter habits for when bottles are unavoidable.

Published: January 15, 2025 12 min read
Standards FDA 21 CFR 165.110 EPA SDWA PNAS 2024 NRDC

Kurz: Americans drink about 16 billion gallons of bottled water a year, but most don't know what "spring," "purified," or "artesian" actually mean on the label. This guide decodes the terms, compares the major brands, explains the FDA rules, and shows you how to drink smarter when bottled water is unavoidable.

Why Bottled Water Became America's Default Drink

In 2023, bottled water passed soda as the most-consumed packaged beverage in the United States for the eighth year running. The Beverage Marketing Corporation pegs annual per-capita consumption at roughly 47 gallons — almost a bottle a day for every American. That shift didn't happen by accident. It started in the 1970s with imported Perrier, accelerated in the 1990s with Aquafina and Dasani, and locked in during the 2000s when single-serve bottles became the default at gas stations, gyms, and airports.

The promise was simple: bottled water is cleaner, safer, and tastes better than what comes out of the tap. That promise is partly true and partly marketing. Some bottled brands really do come from protected springs. Others are filtered municipal water — the same source as your kitchen sink, just packaged. The Natural Resources Defense Council, in a landmark four-year study, found that about 25% of bottled water is essentially repackaged tap water, sometimes with extra treatment, sometimes not.

That doesn't mean bottled water is bad. It means the category is wider and messier than the marketing suggests. If you understand the labels, the sources, and the regulations, you can make better choices — and avoid paying $3 for water that's chemically identical to what you'd get from a city pipe.

The Label Decoded: Spring, Purified, Artesian, Mineral

The FDA, not the EPA, regulates bottled water in the United States. Under 21 CFR 165.110, every term on the label has a legal definition. Most consumers don't know them. Here's the cheat sheet.

Label TermWhat It Legally MeansTypical Brands
Spring waterCollected from an underground formation that flows naturally to the surface. Must be collected at the spring or through a borehole tapping the same formation.Poland Spring, Crystal Geyser, Arrowhead, Deer Park
Purified waterMunicipal or other source water treated by distillation, reverse osmosis, deionization, or another approved process. Must meet US Pharmacopeia purity limits.Aquafina, Dasani, Smartwater (technically vapor-distilled)
Artesian waterFrom a confined aquifer where water rises above the top of the aquifer under natural pressure.Fiji, Voss Artesian
Mineral waterContains at least 250 ppm of total dissolved solids from a protected underground source. No minerals can be added.San Pellegrino, Perrier, Gerolsteiner
Distilled waterVaporized and recondensed. Effectively zero mineral content. Generally not for daily drinking — used in CPAP machines, irons, lab work.Store-brand distilled gallons
Sparkling waterContains carbon dioxide, either natural or added. Source can be spring, mineral, or purified.LaCroix, Topo Chico, Bubly

Two terms that are not legally defined and should raise an eyebrow: "glacier water" and "mountain water." The FDA doesn't regulate them as standards of identity. They're marketing language. A bottle that says "glacier-fresh taste" doesn't have to come within a thousand miles of a glacier.

Reading the Source Statement

Federal law requires bottled water labels to identify the source. Look for it in tiny type, usually near the nutrition panel. If it says "from a municipal source" or "public water supply," that's filtered tap. If it lists a specific spring or aquifer (e.g., "Crystal Springs, Poland, Maine"), it's an actual natural source. This single line tells you more than the front-of-bottle marketing ever will.

How the FDA Regulates Bottled Water

Bottled water is regulated as a packaged food, not as drinking water. That puts it under the FDA, while tap water is regulated by the EPA under the Safe Drinking Water Act. The two agencies set largely parallel standards — but with important differences.

The FDA requires bottlers to test their source water at least once a year for chemical contaminants and weekly for microbiological contaminants like coliform bacteria. Finished product testing happens at least weekly for microbiological quality and yearly for radiological contaminants. Bottlers must keep records and make them available during inspections. The current rules are codified in 21 CFR Part 129 (good manufacturing practices) and 21 CFR 165.110 (quality standards).

The EPA, by contrast, requires public water systems to test for over 90 contaminants, with some — like coliform bacteria — tested hundreds of times per month for large utilities. Public water utilities also have to issue annual Consumer Confidence Reports disclosing exactly what was found. Bottled water has no equivalent disclosure rule. You can't get a CCR for Aquafina.

What's Allowed, What Isn't

Both FDA and EPA allow trace levels of regulated substances: arsenic, lead, nitrates, disinfection byproducts, and dozens more. The numerical limits are nearly identical. Where they differ is enforcement and reporting. The Government Accountability Office and the NRDC have both noted that FDA bottled water inspections are less frequent than EPA tap water audits, and FDA enforcement actions are rare.

One important nuance: bottled water sold across state lines falls under FDA jurisdiction. Bottled water sold only within a single state may be regulated by that state's health department, with rules that vary widely. California, Texas, and Massachusetts have their own active programs; smaller states often defer to federal standards.

The Microplastic Question

A 2024 Columbia University study published in PNAS found that a typical liter of bottled water contains around 240,000 plastic particles, the vast majority being nanoplastics smaller than one micron. The team tested three major US brands. The results varied between brands but every sample contained particles. We cover the methodology and the brand-by-brand picture in detail in Which Bottled Water Brands Have the Most Microplastics?, and the broader research in Microplastics in Bottled Water: What the Studies Actually Show.

Spring Water vs Purified Water: Which Is Better?

This is the question we get most often. The honest answer: it depends on what you're optimizing for.

Spring water contains naturally occurring minerals — calcium, magnesium, bicarbonate, sometimes a little sodium. The mineral profile gives it a distinct taste and contributes a small amount to daily mineral intake. It's not enough to replace a balanced diet, but it's not nothing either.

Purified water has been stripped of nearly all dissolved solids. Reverse osmosis or distillation removes minerals along with contaminants. Some brands re-add a small amount of minerals "for taste" (Dasani adds magnesium sulfate, potassium chloride, and salt). Others, like Aquafina, leave it close to neutral.

Taste, Health, and Practical Differences

  1. Taste: Spring water tends to have a rounder, fuller mouthfeel. Purified water tastes "cleaner" or, to some palates, flat. Mineral water has the most pronounced flavor.
  2. Mineral intake: A liter of typical US spring water contains 50–200 mg of calcium and magnesium combined. That's a small but real contribution. Purified water contributes essentially zero.
  3. Acidity: Purified water often sits at pH 5.5–7.0. Spring waters typically range pH 7.0–8.0. Most people don't notice. People with sensitive teeth occasionally do.
  4. Cooking: Purified water is preferred for coffee, tea, and baby formula because it has predictable mineral content. Specialty coffee shops use either purified water or water remineralized to specific recipes.
  5. Plastic exposure: Both come in plastic bottles unless you buy glass. Both will contain microplastics from the bottle itself, regardless of the water source. This is one reason Reducing Plastic Exposure in Everyday Life matters even when you choose "premium" water.

For everyday drinking, neither is meaningfully healthier than the other for a person eating a normal diet. Choose based on taste, price, and source transparency.

Bottled Water vs Tap Water in the US

About 90% of Americans get tap water from a public utility regulated by the EPA. The quality of that water varies enormously. Some cities — Denver, Minneapolis, San Diego — consistently produce excellent tap water. Others, particularly older systems with lead service lines, have known problems that no national average will capture.

The point isn't that one is better than the other in every case. The point is that "bottled = safer" is a marketing simplification. A clean municipal tap with modern plumbing often produces water that meets or beats a generic purified bottled brand. A municipal tap in a building with lead service lines is a different story. Check your local utility's CCR — it's free and required by law.

Smarter Habits When Bottled Water Is Unavoidable

You can't always control your water source. Airports, road trips, gym vending machines, hotel rooms, conference venues — these are places where the realistic choice is "bottle or thirst." The goal isn't to feel guilty about it. The goal is to make the next bottle a little better than the last.

Eight Practical Habits

  1. Read the source line. If you're paying premium prices, check that you're getting premium source water. Spring or artesian water sourced near where you live tends to be the best value.
  2. Avoid bottles left in heat. Heat accelerates plastic leaching. A case of water that lived in a hot trunk for a week is a different product than the same case from a climate-controlled warehouse. The American Chemistry Council and independent researchers both confirm temperature affects migration of bottle compounds.
  3. Choose larger formats when possible. A single 1-liter bottle has less plastic-to-water ratio than two 500-mL bottles. Less surface area means less particle release per ounce.
  4. Don't refill single-use PET bottles repeatedly. They aren't designed for it. Mechanical wear and microbial growth both increase with reuse. Buy a real reusable bottle for refilling.
  5. Cold-chain your water. Refrigerated bottled water is consistently lower in particle release than warm-stored water from the same brand.
  6. Use a cap-style filter when you have to drink from the bottle. A removable filter that screws onto a standard plastic bottle (DIN 1881 thread, the standard size in the US) lets you remove microplastics from water you've already paid for.   7. Pick glass when it's offered. Restaurants increasingly offer mineral water in glass. The price is similar, the experience is better, and the microplastic load drops dramatically.
  8. Stack a reusable bottle in your daily routine. The single highest-impact change is the one that's free: carry a refillable bottle. It eliminates the bottled-water decision on most days. We cover the on-the-go options in our 2026 portable water filter picks.

The Real Costs: Money, Plastic, Carbon

Bottled water is one of the most expensive ways to hydrate. At $1.50 for a 16.9 oz bottle, you're paying roughly $11 per gallon. Tap water from a typical US municipal utility costs about half a cent per gallon. That's a 2,000-fold price difference for a product that, in many cases, started as the same municipal water you're avoiding.

The plastic footprint matters too. The Pacific Institute estimates that producing a single 1-liter bottle of water requires about 3 liters of water and 0.25 liters of oil, when you account for manufacturing the bottle, packaging, transportation, and refrigeration. The EPA reports that PET bottles have a US recycling rate of around 29%, meaning roughly 7 in 10 end up in landfills, incinerators, or the environment.

None of this means "never buy bottled water." It means be deliberate. Use it when it's the right tool — travel, emergencies, places without safe taps. Skip it when a refill would do the same job for a fraction of the cost.

What Bottled Water Doesn't Solve

One of the persistent myths is that switching to bottled water solves the microplastic problem. It doesn't. Multiple peer-reviewed studies — the 2018 Orb Media / SUNY Fredonia study, the 2024 Columbia nanoplastics study, and ongoing research summarized at Wikipedia's microplastics overview — show that bottled water generally contains more microplastic particles than treated tap water, not fewer.

The reason is mechanical. The bottle, the cap, and the manufacturing process all shed particles into the water. The longer water sits in plastic, the more it picks up. Squeezing a bottle (as most people do) pumps additional particles loose. So if microplastic exposure is your concern, switching brands won't fix it. The fix is either glass packaging, treated tap, or filtration applied to whatever water you're actually drinking.

Bottom Line

Bottled water in America is neither the villain it's sometimes painted as nor the premium product its marketing suggests. It's a regulated category with real differences between brands, sources, and treatment methods — and real similarities between bottles whose price tags differ by 5x. Read the source line. Know whether you're drinking spring water from Maine or filtered tap from a regional bottling plant. And when bottled water is unavoidable, take the small steps that actually matter: cold storage, larger formats, a refillable when you can, a filter when you can't. FAQ

Is bottled water safer than tap water in the US?

Not categorically. EPA-regulated tap water from a well-maintained municipal system often meets or beats generic purified bottled water. Bottled water can be a better choice if your home plumbing has lead service lines or your local Consumer Confidence Report shows recurring violations. For most Americans on modern plumbing, the difference is taste and convenience, not safety.

What does "purified water" actually mean on a label?

Under FDA rules in 21 CFR 165.110, purified water has been treated by distillation, reverse osmosis, deionization, or another approved process to meet US Pharmacopeia purity limits. The source is usually municipal tap water. Brands like Aquafina, Dasani, and Smartwater all start with public water supplies before treatment.

Does bottled water contain microplastics?

Yes. A 2024 Columbia University study published in PNAS found roughly 240,000 plastic particles per liter in tested US bottled water, mostly nanoplastics under one micron. The bottle, cap, and manufacturing process all shed particles. Switching brands does not solve this; filtration or glass packaging does.

Why is spring water more expensive than purified water?

Spring water is collected from a specific underground source and transported to bottling plants, which adds logistics costs. Purified water can be produced anywhere municipal water is available, so bottling plants are spread across the country. The price difference reflects sourcing and transport, not necessarily quality.

Can I refill a single-use plastic water bottle?

It is not recommended. Single-use PET bottles are not designed for repeated washing or reuse. Mechanical wear increases microplastic shedding, and incomplete cleaning allows microbial growth. Use a bottle designed for reuse, ideally stainless steel or thick-walled BPA-free plastic.

Is sparkling water regulated the same as still bottled water?

Sparkling bottled water falls under the same FDA bottled water rules in 21 CFR 165.110 if labeled as a bottled water product. Some sparkling waters are classified as soft drinks instead, which changes the labeling and disclosure requirements. Check the front label to see whether it is described as sparkling water or as a flavored beverage.
